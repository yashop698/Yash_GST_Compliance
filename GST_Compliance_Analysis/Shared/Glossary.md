## Purpose and Scope

This glossary provides precise, legally grounded definitions for all key terms used across the three GST compliance topics covered in this analysis: Reverse Charge Mechanism (RCM) under Sections 9(3) and 9(4) of the CGST Act and Sections 5(3) and 5(4) of the IGST Act; Blocked Input Tax Credit under Section 17(5) of the CGST Act read with Schedule III; and ITC Apportionment under Rule 43 of the CGST Rules, 2017 applicable to taxpayers making both taxable and exempt supplies. The definitions herein are drawn from the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017; the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017; the Maharashtra Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017; the CGST Rules, 2017; and relevant CBIC circulars, notifications, and judicial pronouncements as in force up to May 2026. This glossary is intended for use by Chartered Accountants, Company Secretaries, and Cost and Management Accountants engaged in GST compliance, advisory, audit, and litigation support work.

## Statutory vs Practical Definitions

Several terms in this glossary carry a precise statutory definition under the CGST Act, 2017 or the CGST Rules, 2017 — for example, "Exempt Supply" (Section 2(47)), "Taxable Supply" (Section 2(108)), "Input Tax Credit" (Section 2(63)), and "Reverse Charge" (Section 2(98)). These statutory definitions are authoritative and must be applied in their exact legislative form for compliance and litigation purposes. Other terms — such as "Partial Exemption," "Common Input," and "Ineligible Supply" (in the apportionment context) — are used extensively in practice and in CBIC circulars and ICAI guidance notes, but do not have a dedicated statutory definition. For such terms, the definitions below represent the settled practical and judicial understanding as of May 2026. Where the practical meaning of a term diverges from or is broader than its strict statutory definition, that distinction is explicitly noted. Professionals must exercise particular caution with terms like "Exempt Supply," which carries a wider meaning for ITC purposes under Section 17(3) than its standalone definition in Section 2(47).

## Terms

### Apportionment
{law_reference: "Rule 43 CGST Rules, 2017; Section 17(2) CGST Act, 2017"}

Apportionment, in the GST context, is the process of dividing the Input Tax Credit (ITC) on goods and services that are used simultaneously for both taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) and exempt supplies, in proportion to their respective use, so as to determine the portion of ITC that is eligible for credit and the portion that must be reversed. It is the legislative mechanism for giving effect to the principle enshrined in Section 17(2) of the CGST Act, 2017, which provides that where goods or services or both are used partly for the purposes of business and partly for other purposes, or partly for making taxable supplies and partly for making exempt supplies, the amount of credit shall be restricted to so much of the input tax as is attributable to taxable supplies. Rule 43 of the CGST Rules, 2017 operationalises Section 17(2) by prescribing separate computation methodologies for capital goods and for inputs and input services.

Apportionment must be distinguished from "attribution," which is the prior step: attribution involves identifying which inputs are exclusively attributable to taxable supplies, which are exclusively attributable to exempt supplies, and which are common to both (i.e., cannot be directly attributed to either). Only the common portion proceeds to the apportionment stage; exclusively attributable inputs are dealt with directly (ITC allowed on exclusively taxable inputs; ITC fully denied on exclusively exempt inputs). Apportionment thus applies only to common inputs and input services (and in a modified form to capital goods under Rule 43(1)). The apportionment exercise is a monthly exercise for inputs and input services and involves an annual true-up adjustment under Rule 43(2) / Rule 42(2), reportable in GSTR-9.

### Apportionment Ratio
{law_reference: "Rule 43(1)(c) CGST Rules, 2017; Rule 42(1)(g) and (h) CGST Rules, 2017"}

The Apportionment Ratio is the fraction used to determine what proportion of the ITC on common inputs and input services must be reversed (i.e., is attributable to exempt supplies and therefore ineligible). Under Rule 42(1)(h), the ratio is computed as:

**Apportionment Ratio = Aggregate value of exempt supplies during the tax period / Total turnover in the State during the tax period**

- **Numerator:** The aggregate value of exempt supplies made from the State during the tax period. For this purpose, "exempt supply" under Section 17(3) includes nil-rated supplies, supplies that are wholly exempt under the CGST Act or IGST Act or any notification issued thereunder, and transactions specified in Schedule III (treated as neither supply of goods nor supply of services). Notably, the value of export supplies (zero-rated) and value of supplies to SEZs are excluded from this numerator.
- **Denominator:** The total turnover in the State during the tax period, i.e., the aggregate value of all supplies made from the State, including taxable supplies, exempt supplies, and zero-rated exports, but computed as per Section 2(112) of the CGST Act (total turnover). Turnover in State is defined in Section 2(112).

For capital goods under Rule 43(1)(c), a similar ratio is applied but is computed on a monthly basis across a 60-month useful life, where the ITC attributable to exempt supplies each month = (1/60th of total ITC on capital goods) × (Exempt Turnover / Total Turnover for that month).

The Apportionment Ratio may be computed provisionally each tax period and is subject to annual adjustment at the end of the financial year under Rule 42(2), where the actual annual ratio is compared with the aggregate of the monthly provisional ratios, and any difference is paid or credited with interest.

### Blocked ITC (Section 17(5))
{law_reference: "Section 17(5) CGST Act, 2017"}

Blocked ITC refers to the categories of Input Tax Credit that are expressly denied by statute under Section 17(5) of the CGST Act, 2017, notwithstanding that the recipient of the supply would otherwise satisfy all the conditions for claiming ITC under Section 16. Section 17(5) represents an absolute statutory bar — it is not a consequence of apportionment or partial exemption but rather a specific legislative decision to deny credit on defined categories of inward supplies, typically on grounds of potential misuse, definitional complexity, or policy considerations.

The full list of categories under Section 17(5) (as amended up to May 2026) includes:
**(a)** Motor vehicles for transportation of persons having approved seating capacity of not more than 13 persons (including the driver), except where they are used for: (i) further supply of such motor vehicles; (ii) transportation of passengers; (iii) imparting training on driving such vehicles. This clause was substantially amended by the Finance Act, 2021, effective 1 January 2022, to reinstate the capacity-based threshold that had been disrupted by prior amendments.
**(aa)** Vessels and aircraft, subject to similar exceptions as in (a) relating to further supply, transportation, and training.
**(ab)** Services of general insurance, servicing, repair and maintenance in relation to motor vehicles covered under (a), vessels and aircraft covered under (aa) — when the underlying motor vehicle/vessel/aircraft ITC is itself blocked.
**(b)** Food and beverages, outdoor catering, beauty treatment, health services, cosmetic and plastic surgery, leasing, renting or hiring of motor vehicles (other than those covered under (a)), life insurance, health insurance — except where used to make outward taxable supplies of the same category or as an element of composite/mixed supply.
**(c)** Works contract services when supplied for construction of an immovable property (other than plant and machinery), even when received for the purpose of business. Exception: where the input service is for further supply of works contract services.
**(d)** Goods or services or both received by a taxable person for construction of an immovable property on his own account, including when such goods or services are used in the course or furtherance of business. Exception: plant and machinery.
**(e)** Goods or services or both on which tax has been paid under Section 10 (Composition Scheme) — i.e., composition dealers cannot claim ITC.
**(f)** Goods or services received by a non-resident taxable person, except on goods imported by him.
**(g)** Goods or services used for personal consumption.
**(h)** Goods lost, stolen, destroyed, written off, or disposed of by way of gift or free samples.
**(i)** Any tax paid in accordance with Sections 74, 129, and 130 (tax paid on account of fraud, misdeclaration, confiscation, etc.).

Blocked ITC fundamentally differs from ineligible ITC arising through apportionment under Rule 43 / Rule 42: the latter is a proportional, formula-driven reduction that can fluctuate month to month depending on the taxpayer's exempt/taxable output ratio, whereas blocked ITC under Section 17(5) is an absolute prohibition regardless of the taxpayer's output mix. A taxpayer engaged wholly in taxable supplies still cannot claim ITC on motor vehicles used for executive transportation (which falls under Section 17(5)(a)), just as a taxpayer making 100% exempt supplies cannot claim ITC under either provision.

### Common Input
{law_reference: "Rule 43(1)(c) CGST Rules, 2017; Rule 42(1)(c) and (d) CGST Rules, 2017"}

A "common input" (or "common input service" in the case of services) is an inward supply of goods or services or both that is used, or intended to be used, for making both taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies) and exempt supplies by the registered person, and that cannot be directly and exclusively attributed to either category of outward supply. The concept of "common input" is not defined by name in the statute, but is clearly contemplated by Rule 42(1)(c) and (d) which distinguish between:
- **(T1):** ITC attributable exclusively to taxable supplies (including zero-rated) — fully eligible.
- **(T2):** ITC attributable exclusively to exempt supplies — fully ineligible.
- **(T3):** ITC attributable to inputs and input services intended for use partly for business and partly for non-business purposes — to be reversed proportionately.
- **(C3):** The remaining ITC, i.e., ITC on common inputs and input services not falling in any of the above categories — subject to apportionment under Rule 42(1)(f), (g), and (h).

Common inputs are therefore inputs and input services that serve multiple output streams without the possibility of direct attribution. Classic examples include: electricity for a factory producing both taxable and exempt products; accountancy, legal, and secretarial services procured at head office level; rent for common office space; IT infrastructure supporting both taxable and exempt business lines. The key test for "commonality" is whether the supplier's invoice relates to an activity that feeds into both taxable and exempt supply chains — if so, the ITC thereon is "common" and must proceed through the apportionment formula under Rule 42 (for inputs and input services) or Rule 43 (for capital goods).

Common inputs must be distinguished from "directly attributable inputs": if an input is used exclusively for a taxable supply, its ITC is fully claimable without apportionment; if exclusively for an exempt supply, it is entirely blocked under Section 17(2) without reference to any formula. The apportionment mechanism under Rules 42 and 43 is triggered only for the common pool.

### Deemed Supplier
{law_reference: "Section 9(3) CGST Act, 2017; Section 9(4) CGST Act, 2017; Section 5(3) IGST Act, 2017; relevant CBIC notifications including Notification No. 13/2017-CT(Rate) dated 28-Jun-2017"}

The concept of a "Deemed Supplier" arises in the context of the Reverse Charge Mechanism (RCM). Under the normal GST mechanism, the supplier of goods or services is responsible for collecting and remitting GST. Under RCM (Sections 9(3) and 9(4)), this liability is shifted to the recipient. However, for certain administrative and compliance purposes — particularly in the context of invoice issuance, time of supply, and place of supply — the recipient is treated as if it were the supplier, or alternatively the original supplier is "deemed" to have supplied the goods or services through a specified intermediary.

In the specific context of Section 9(3) RCM on notified services (such as Goods Transport Agency services, advocate services, director services, import of services), where the actual supplier is a GTA, an advocate, a director, or an overseas service provider, the GST liability is cast on the recipient who is a registered business entity. The original supplier is sometimes referred to as the "deemed supplier" in the sense that although the recipient pays the tax, the supply is still deemed to have originated from that supplier for purposes of computing time of supply (Section 13, CGST Act) and determining the nature of supply.

Additionally, "deemed supplier" terminology arises in the e-commerce context under Section 9(5), where an e-commerce operator is deemed to be the supplier for specified services (restaurant services, passenger transport, housekeeping services) and is liable to pay GST in lieu of the actual supplier. This is a distinct usage of the concept.

In Maharashtra, the MGST Act mirrors these provisions through Section 9(3) and Section 9(4) of the MGST Act, 2017, with identical deeming provisions. Professionals must carefully distinguish between "deemed supplier" under RCM (where the recipient pays but the supply is attributed to the original supplier) and "deemed supply" under Schedule I (which creates supply without consideration for specific related-party transactions).

### Eligible Supply (GST-Taxable)
{law_reference: "Section 2(108) CGST Act, 2017"}

"Taxable supply" is defined under Section 2(108) of the CGST Act, 2017 as a supply of goods or services or both which is leviable to tax under this Act. For the purposes of ITC eligibility and apportionment, "eligible supply" or "GST-taxable supply" refers to a supply on which output tax is actually payable (at rates other than NIL) and which is not exempt, nil-rated, or non-taxable.

The following distinctions are critical for apportionment and ITC eligibility purposes:
- **Taxable supply (standard rated):** Supplies where GST is payable at 5%, 12%, 18%, or 28% — fully eligible; ITC on inputs for such supplies is fully claimable.
- **Zero-rated supply (exports / SEZ):** Treated as taxable but with output tax at 0%; ITC on inputs for zero-rated supplies under Section 16(1) of the IGST Act is fully eligible (and refundable). Zero-rated supplies are specifically excluded from the "exempt supply" definition under Section 17(3) for ITC purposes.
- **Nil-rated supply:** Supplies specifically notified at 0% rate — treated as exempt supplies for ITC apportionment purposes under Section 17(3); ITC on inputs exclusively attributable to nil-rated supplies is blocked.
- **Exempt supply:** Wholly exempt by notification — treated as exempt for ITC purposes; ITC denied proportionately.
- **Non-taxable supply:** Supply of petroleum crude, high speed diesel, motor spirit, natural gas, aviation turbine fuel, and alcoholic liquor for human consumption — not subject to GST; ITC denied.

A supply is "eligible" for ITC purposes when it is taxable (standard-rated or zero-rated) and the ITC conditions of Section 16 are met. Professionals must be careful not to confuse "taxable" (Section 2(108)) with "eligible" for ITC — a supply can be taxable but the related ITC may still be blocked under Section 17(5).

### Exempt Supply
{law_reference: "Section 2(47) CGST Act, 2017; Section 17(3) CGST Act, 2017"}

"Exempt supply" has two operative definitions under the CGST Act, and the distinction between them is critically important for ITC apportionment purposes.

**Section 2(47) — Standalone definition:** "Exempt supply" means supply of any goods or services or both which attracts nil rate of tax or which may be wholly exempt from tax under Section 11 of the CGST Act or under Section 6 of the IGST Act, and includes non-taxable supply. Under this definition, exempt supply = nil-rated + wholly exempt + non-taxable.

**Section 17(3) — ITC apportionment definition (wider):** For the purposes of sub-section (2) of Section 17 (i.e., for ITC apportionment), the value of exempt supply shall include:
1. Supplies that are wholly exempt from tax;
2. Supplies that attract nil rate of tax;
3. Supplies that are not leviable to tax under the GST Acts (non-taxable supplies, i.e., alcohol, petroleum products);
4. Transactions specified in Schedule III of the CGST Act, 2017 (activities/transactions treated as neither supply of goods nor supply of services).

Critically, under the proviso to Section 17(3), the value of supply of securities shall be one percent of the value of outward supply of securities for the purposes of the denominator of the apportionment formula — a provision particularly relevant to NBFCs, stock brokers, and holding companies dealing in securities.

**What is NOT exempt supply for ITC purposes:** Zero-rated supplies (exports and SEZ supplies under Section 16(1) IGST Act) are expressly excluded from the definition of "exempt supply" under Section 17(3) for ITC apportionment purposes. This is the most practically significant feature of Section 17(3): an exporter making 100% zero-rated supplies can claim full ITC without any apportionment, even though the output tax rate is 0%, because zero-rated supplies are not "exempt supplies" under Section 17(3).

The broader Section 17(3) definition is the one that must be used when computing the Apportionment Ratio numerator under Rule 42 and Rule 43. Professionals who use the narrower Section 2(47) definition for this purpose commit a compliance error.

### Ineligible Supply
{law_reference: "Section 17(3) CGST Act, 2017; Section 17(2) CGST Act, 2017"}

"Ineligible supply" is a practical term (not a term of art under the CGST Act) used in the apportionment context to collectively refer to the category of supplies in respect of which ITC on common inputs cannot be claimed. For Rule 42 and Rule 43 apportionment purposes, the "ineligible" category of supplies comprises those that fall within the extended definition of "exempt supply" under Section 17(3), namely:

1. Supplies wholly exempt from GST (by notification under Section 11 CGST Act or Section 6 IGST Act);
2. Nil-rated supplies (attract 0% GST by rate notification);
3. Non-taxable supplies (petroleum crude, ATF, natural gas, alcohol — outside GST levy altogether);
4. Schedule III transactions (e.g., actionable claims other than lottery/gambling, employee services to employer, sale of land, sale of completed buildings — per relevant judicial decisions).

The value of such "ineligible supplies" forms the numerator of the Apportionment Ratio under Rule 42(1)(g), and the ITC attributable to them must be reversed.

**Critical exclusion — Zero-rated supplies are NOT ineligible:** Export supplies and SEZ supplies that are zero-rated under Section 16 of the IGST Act are not "ineligible" for ITC purposes. The ITC on inputs/input services used for making zero-rated supplies is fully claimable, and these supplies must not be included in the numerator of the apportionment ratio. This is a frequent error in practice.

**Blocked ITC vs Ineligible ITC:** Ineligibility through apportionment (Rule 42/43) is output-ratio driven and may change month to month. Blocked ITC under Section 17(5) is category-driven and absolute. The two are independent denial mechanisms — even if apportionment yields zero reversal (because no exempt supplies are made), ITC on Section 17(5) items remains blocked.

### Input Tax Credit (ITC)
{law_reference: "Section 2(63) CGST Act, 2017; Section 16 CGST Act, 2017; Section 17 CGST Act, 2017"}

"Input Tax Credit" is defined under Section 2(63) of the CGST Act, 2017 as the credit of input tax, meaning the central tax, State tax, integrated tax, or Union territory tax charged on any supply of goods or services or both made to a registered person. Input tax also includes the tax payable under RCM (reverse charge) under Sections 9(3) and 9(4), and IGST paid on imports (Section 2(62)). It does not include the tax paid under Section 10 (composition levy).

**Four mandatory conditions for ITC eligibility under Section 16(2):**
1. **Possession of a valid tax invoice or debit note** issued by a supplier registered under the CGST Act (or specified document under Rule 36 for RCM self-invoices, bill of entry for imports, etc.);
2. **Receipt of goods or services or both** — for goods supplied in installments, ITC is available only upon receipt of the last installment; for services, upon receipt as per the contract;
3. **Tax has actually been paid by the supplier to the government** — (as confirmed by the matching of GSTR-2B / GSTR-2A), so that the supplier has discharged the GST liability reflected on the invoice. The Finance Act, 2021 retrospectively inserted this condition to align it with the GSTR-2B matching mechanism;
4. **Return filed by the recipient** — the recipient must have furnished his return under Section 39 (GSTR-3B).

**Additional conditions under Section 16(3) and (4):**
- Where depreciation has been claimed on the tax component of the cost of capital goods, ITC is not available on such tax (Section 16(3)).
- ITC cannot be taken after the due date for filing of the return for the month of November following the end of the financial year to which the invoice relates, or after filing of the annual return, whichever is earlier (Section 16(4)). This is the time-limit for ITC claims.

**Conditional eligibility — Blocked ITC, Apportionment:** Even where all Section 16 conditions are met, ITC may be partially or wholly denied if: (a) the goods or services fall under Section 17(5) — absolute block; or (b) the goods or services are used partly for exempt supplies — proportionate denial under Rules 42/43.

**ITC on RCM:** Where tax is paid by the recipient under RCM (Sections 9(3)/9(4)), the recipient is entitled to avail ITC of the tax paid (subject to all Section 16 conditions and Section 17 restrictions), but only after actual payment of the RCM tax in cash to the government. The ITC on RCM can be availed in the same return period in which RCM is paid, provided all conditions are met. However, ITC on RCM cannot be used to pay the RCM liability itself (Section 49(4) read with CBIC clarifications).

### Mixed Supply
{law_reference: "Section 2(74) CGST Act, 2017"}

"Mixed supply" is defined under Section 2(74) of the CGST Act, 2017 as two or more individual supplies of goods or services or any combination thereof, made in conjunction with each other by a taxable person for a single price where such supply does not constitute a composite supply. The key characteristics of a mixed supply are:
1. **Two or more individual supplies** that are capable of being supplied independently;
2. **Supplied together for a single price** — the combination is for commercial convenience rather than natural bundling;
3. **No principal supply** that can be identified — unlike a composite supply where there is always a dominant element;
4. **Tax treatment:** A mixed supply is taxed at the rate applicable to the supply which attracts the highest rate of GST among all the components.

The critical distinguishing factor from a composite supply (Section 2(30)) is that in a mixed supply, the individual components are not naturally bundled and do not lose their independent identity — they are merely sold together for price convenience. A hamper of chocolates, dry fruits, and wine sold as a gift basket, or a combination of taxable and exempt goods sold at a single price, may constitute a mixed supply. The practical importance for ITC is that a mixed supply attracts GST at the highest applicable rate, which may make an otherwise exempt component taxable (e.g., if one component attracts 28%, the whole mixed supply is taxed at 28%).

For ITC apportionment purposes, where a supplier makes mixed supplies including exempt components, the characterisation as mixed supply may eliminate the need for apportionment on the input side because the entire supply value becomes taxable at the highest rate — but this analysis depends on whether the transaction truly qualifies as a mixed supply versus a situation where exempt and taxable supplies are merely invoiced together.

### Partial Exemption
{law_reference: "Section 17(2) CGST Act, 2017; Rule 42 CGST Rules, 2017; Rule 43 CGST Rules, 2017"}

"Partial exemption" is a practical term (not used in the CGST Act or Rules by this name) that describes the situation where a GST-registered person makes both taxable supplies and exempt supplies simultaneously, resulting in a position where ITC on common inputs cannot be claimed in full but must be apportioned. The term "partial" reflects the fact that the registered person is partially in the GST mainstream (for taxable supplies) and partially outside it (for exempt supplies).

The consequence of partial exemption for ITC is governed by Section 17(2) and implemented through Rule 42 (for inputs and input services) and Rule 43 (for capital goods):
- **Monthly provisional reversal:** Each month, the taxpayer must compute the portion of common ITC attributable to exempt supplies using the Apportionment Ratio and reverse that amount in GSTR-3B.
- **Annual true-up:** At the end of each financial year, the taxpayer must compare the aggregate of monthly reversals with the actual annual ratio-based reversal. Any shortfall must be paid with interest; any excess can be claimed back as credit. This annual adjustment is to be effected in the return for September following the end of the financial year or in the annual return (GSTR-9), whichever is filed earlier.
- **Partial exemption is not an issue for Section 17(5) blocks:** Partial exemption and the resulting apportionment do not override or interact with Section 17(5) blocks — blocked ITC under Section 17(5) is separately denied regardless of the taxable/exempt output ratio.

Partial exemption situations are extremely common in practice: banks and financial institutions (exempt financial services and taxable advisory/processing services), real estate developers (exempt residential sales post-completion and taxable construction stage), hospitals (exempt healthcare and taxable pharmacy/cosmetic procedures), educational institutions (exempt education and taxable commercial training).

### Proportionate Reduction
{law_reference: "Section 17(2) CGST Act, 2017; Rule 43 CGST Rules, 2017; Rule 42 CGST Rules, 2017"}

Proportionate reduction is the mechanism prescribed by Section 17(2) of the CGST Act, 2017 — implemented through Rules 42 and 43 — by which the ITC on common inputs (inputs, input services, and capital goods used for both taxable and exempt supplies) is reduced in proportion to the value of exempt supplies relative to total turnover. The mechanism ensures that the GST law does not allow a registered person to obtain the benefit of ITC for the portion of inputs consumed in making supplies on which no output tax is collected (exempt supplies).

**Computation under Rule 42 (inputs and input services):**
Step 1 — Identify total ITC available (T) = T1 + T2 + T3 + T4 (where T1 is for taxable supplies; T2 for exempt; T3 for non-business; T4 = T - T1 - T2 - T3 = common ITC = C2 after adjustments).
Step 2 — Compute D1 = C2 × (Exempt Turnover / Total Turnover) = ITC to be reversed proportionately.
Step 3 — C3 (eligible common ITC) = C2 - D1.
Step 4 — Annual adjustment under Rule 42(2) to true up monthly provisional ratios.

**Computation under Rule 43 (capital goods):**
1/60th of total ITC on each capital good is computed monthly (reflecting the 5-year useful life assumption).
The monthly ITC on capital goods attributable to exempt supplies = (1/60th of total ITC) × (Exempt Turnover / Total Turnover for that month).
This monthly attribution is accumulated and reversed annually.

**Practical significance:** Proportionate reduction ensures that businesses do not fully benefit from ITC on overhead inputs (like rent, electricity, accounting services) when a portion of their business outputs are exempt. The formula uses turnover as a proxy for input consumption — recognising that exact attribution of common overheads is often impractical.

### Reverse Charge Mechanism (RCM)
{law_reference: "Section 9(3) CGST Act, 2017; Section 9(4) CGST Act, 2017; Section 5(3) IGST Act, 2017; Section 5(4) IGST Act, 2017; Section 2(98) CGST Act, 2017"}

"Reverse charge" is defined in Section 2(98) of the CGST Act, 2017 as the liability to pay tax by the recipient of the supply of goods or services or both instead of the supplier of such goods or services or both under sub-section (3) or sub-section (4) of Section 9 of the CGST Act, or under sub-section (3) or sub-section (4) of Section 5 of the IGST Act. The Reverse Charge Mechanism (RCM) is therefore the statutory framework under which the tax liability on a supply is shifted from the supplier to the recipient.

**Section 9(3) — Mandatory RCM on notified supplies (Category-based RCM):** Under Section 9(3), the Government may, on recommendation of the GST Council, notify specific categories of goods and services on which the recipient (if registered) is liable to pay GST instead of the supplier. The most significant services notified under Section 9(3) as of May 2026 include:
- Goods Transport Agency (GTA) services for transportation of goods by road — recipient being a body corporate, registered person, factory, co-operative society, or government body;
- Legal services provided by an individual advocate (including senior advocate) or a firm of advocates to a business entity;
- Services of a director of a company or body corporate to the company;
- Services provided by insurance agents to an insurance company;
- Import of services — governed by Section 7(4) (deemed intra-State supply) and Section 5(3) of the IGST Act;
- Sponsorship services provided to a body corporate or partnership firm;
- Government-related services (renting of immovable property by Central/State Government to business entities where exempt notification does not apply);
- Overriding royalty and copyright services.

**Section 9(4) — RCM on supplies from unregistered suppliers:** Under the original Section 9(4), any registered person who received supplies from an unregistered supplier was liable to pay GST on RCM. This provision was suspended in 2018 and then partially restored in 2019 (see Amendments Timeline). As of May 2026, Section 9(4) applies only to specified categories of goods and services as notified by the Government — not to all unregistered supplies.

**IGST equivalents:** Sections 5(3) and 5(4) of the IGST Act, 2017 are the inter-State equivalents of Sections 9(3) and 9(4) respectively, and apply the same RCM principles to inter-State supplies.

**Compliance requirements under RCM:**
1. The recipient must issue a self-invoice (payment voucher) under Rule 36 for each RCM supply;
2. The recipient must pay the RCM tax in cash (electronic cash ledger) — RCM liability cannot be offset using ITC;
3. The recipient can claim ITC of RCM tax paid, subject to Section 16 conditions and Section 17 restrictions;
4. The recipient must report RCM outward liability in Table 3.1(d) of GSTR-3B and inward supply details in GSTR-2B;
5. The time of supply for RCM purposes is governed by the earlier of: date of payment, or 60 days from date of supply (for goods) / date of issuance of invoice or date of payment (for services) — per Sections 12 and 13 of the CGST Act.

**Maharashtra application:** The MGST Act, 2017 mirrors Sections 9(3) and 9(4) through identical provisions, and MGST RCM notifications follow the Central Government notifications with Maharashtra-specific adaptations where applicable.

### Unregistered Supplier
{law_reference: "Section 9(4) CGST Act, 2017; Section 22 CGST Act, 2017; Section 23 CGST Act, 2017"}

An "unregistered supplier" is a person who makes a taxable supply of goods or services but is not registered under the CGST Act — either because his aggregate turnover is below the threshold for compulsory registration (currently Rs. 40 lakhs for goods, Rs. 20 lakhs for services in most States including Maharashtra; Rs. 20 lakhs for goods and Rs. 10 lakhs in specified Special Category States), or because he is exempt from registration under Section 23, or because he has not complied with registration requirements despite being liable.

**Registration thresholds (Maharashtra as of May 2026):** Maharashtra is not a Special Category State, so the standard thresholds apply: Rs. 40 lakhs for exclusive supply of goods; Rs. 20 lakhs for supply of services or mixed supply. A person exclusively supplying goods that are exempt from GST is not required to register, even if turnover exceeds these limits (per Section 23(1)(a)).

**RCM on unregistered suppliers:** Where a registered taxpayer receives specified supplies from an unregistered supplier, RCM under Section 9(4) is triggered (for the notified categories). In such cases, the registered recipient must:
1. Self-assess and pay GST on the value of supply at the applicable rate;
2. Issue a payment voucher (self-invoice);
3. Claim ITC of the RCM tax paid (subject to Section 16/17 conditions);
4. Report in GSTR-3B Table 3.1(d).

**Composition dealers and unregistered status:** A composition dealer (Section 10) is registered but cannot issue a tax invoice or collect GST from the recipient — for RCM purposes, a composition dealer is treated differently from an unregistered supplier (the recipient does not pay RCM on supplies from a composition dealer, but the composition dealer himself is subject to composition levy).

**Identification challenge:** In practice, identifying whether a supplier is registered (and validly registered with an active GSTIN) requires verification on the GST Portal. Supplies from a supplier with a cancelled or suspended GSTIN may be treated as supplies from an unregistered supplier for RCM purposes if the cancellation is effective from a date before the supply.

## Additional Terms

### Composite Supply
{law_reference: "Section 2(30) CGST Act, 2017"}

"Composite supply" is defined under Section 2(30) of the CGST Act, 2017 as a supply made by a taxable person to a recipient consisting of two or more taxable supplies of goods or services or both, or any combination thereof, which are naturally bundled and supplied in conjunction with each other in the ordinary course of business, one of which is a principal supply. A composite supply is taxed at the rate applicable to the principal supply (the dominant element of the bundle) and is treated as a single supply for all GST purposes. The classic example is a hotel stay that includes room accommodation (principal supply) along with meals, laundry, and internet — these are naturally bundled and the accommodation rate governs the whole composite supply. For ITC purposes, the character of inputs used in making the principal supply governs ITC eligibility for the composite supply as a whole.

### Zero-Rated Supply
{law_reference: "Section 16(1) IGST Act, 2017; Section 2(23) CGST Act, 2017"}

"Zero-rated supply" is defined in Section 2(23) of the CGST Act (by reference to Section 16 of the IGST Act). Section 16(1) of the IGST Act, 2017 provides that "zero-rated supply" means export of goods or services or both, or supply of goods or services or both to a Special Economic Zone (SEZ) developer or SEZ unit. Zero-rated supplies are charged at 0% output tax, yet the supplier is entitled to full ITC on inputs and input services used in making zero-rated supplies — either by supplying under LUT/bond (and then claiming refund of accumulated ITC) or by paying IGST and claiming refund thereof. This is the fundamental difference from a nil-rated or exempt supply, where ITC is denied. For apportionment purposes, zero-rated supplies are explicitly excluded from "exempt supply" under Section 17(3), meaning they do not reduce the ITC available to exporters or SEZ suppliers.

### Taxable Person
{law_reference: "Section 2(107) CGST Act, 2017"}

"Taxable person" is defined under Section 2(107) of the CGST Act, 2017 as a person who is registered or liable to be registered under Section 22 or Section 24 of the Act. Section 22 provides for registration on the basis of aggregate turnover threshold; Section 24 provides for mandatory registration irrespective of turnover (for inter-State suppliers, e-commerce operators, casual taxable persons, non-resident taxable persons, etc.). A "taxable person" is distinct from a "registered person" — every registered person is a taxable person, but the reverse is not always true (a person liable to be registered but not yet registered is also a taxable person, which has implications for RCM liability and ITC entitlement).

### Composition Dealer
{law_reference: "Section 10 CGST Act, 2017; Rule 7 CGST Rules, 2017"}

A "composition dealer" is a registered taxable person who has opted for the Composition Scheme under Section 10 of the CGST Act, 2017, which allows eligible small taxpayers (aggregate turnover in preceding financial year not exceeding Rs. 1.5 crore for goods; Rs. 75 lakhs for services/mixed suppliers under Section 10(2A)) to pay GST at a fixed percentage of turnover instead of the regular GST rate. Composition dealers: (i) cannot issue tax invoices and cannot collect GST from customers; (ii) cannot claim ITC (Section 17(5)(e) — ITC is blocked for composition dealers); (iii) are required to pay GST from their own pocket at the composition rates; (iv) cannot make inter-State outward supplies. Supplies from a composition dealer carry no input tax that the recipient can claim as ITC, which makes composition dealers' supplies economically similar to exempt supplies for the downstream buyer's ITC perspective.

### Electronic Credit Ledger
{law_reference: "Section 49(2) CGST Act, 2017; Rule 86 CGST Rules, 2017"}

The Electronic Credit Ledger (ECL) is the ledger maintained on the GST Portal for each registered person in which all ITC (Input Tax Credit) available to that person is credited. Under Section 49(2), all ITC under this Act shall be credited to the electronic credit ledger. The ECL is maintained separately for CGST, SGST/UTGST, and IGST credits. The utilisation order from the ECL is governed by Section 49(5) — IGST credit can be utilised first against IGST liability, then CGST, then SGST; CGST credit can be utilised against CGST and IGST (in that order); SGST credit against SGST and IGST (in that order). CGST credit cannot be used against SGST liability and vice versa. In the context of ITC apportionment and blocked ITC, amounts reversed from the ECL under Rule 42/43 are debited to the ECL (reducing the credit balance), and any ITC that was never eligible (blocked ITC) must not be credited to the ECL in the first place or, if erroneously credited, must be reversed.

### Input Service Distributor (ISD)
{law_reference: "Section 2(61) CGST Act, 2017; Rule 39 CGST Rules, 2017"}

"Input Service Distributor" (ISD) is defined under Section 2(61) of the CGST Act, 2017 as an office of the supplier of goods or services or both which receives tax invoices issued under Section 31 towards the receipt of input services and issues a prescribed document for the purpose of distributing the credit of central tax, State tax, integrated tax, or Union territory tax paid on the said services to a supplier of taxable goods or services or both having the same Permanent Account Number as that of the said office. The ISD mechanism is distinct from the regular ITC mechanism — it applies specifically to a head office or centralised procurement function that receives services on behalf of multiple branches/units (with different GSTINs under the same PAN) and needs to distribute the credit to those branches for their use. ISD is particularly relevant in the context of ITC apportionment: the distributed ITC retains its character (CGST/SGST/IGST) and the recipient branch must apply its own apportionment ratio to the distributed ITC if it makes both taxable and exempt supplies. As of 2024, Finance Act amendments have clarified ISD provisions to make ISD mandatory for certain categories of common input services procured centrally.

### Works Contract
{law_reference: "Section 2(119) CGST Act, 2017"}

"Works contract" is defined under Section 2(119) of the CGST Act, 2017 as a contract for building, construction, fabrication, completion, erection, installation, fitting out, improvement, modification, repair, maintenance, renovation, alteration, or commissioning of any immovable property wherein transfer of property in goods (whether as goods or in some other form) is involved in the execution of such contract. Works contract services under GST are treated as supply of services (not goods) and are taxed accordingly. The definition is critical for ITC purposes because Section 17(5)(c) blocks ITC on works contract services received for construction of immovable property (other than plant and machinery), and Section 17(5)(d) blocks ITC on goods/services used for self-construction of immovable property. Works contracts for plant and machinery are specifically exempted from the ITC block — making the classification of the immovable property as "building" versus "plant and machinery" a significant compliance issue.

### Plant and Machinery
{law_reference: "Explanation to Section 17 CGST Act, 2017; Explanation 2 to Section 17(5)(d)"}

"Plant and machinery" is defined in the Explanation appended to Section 17 of the CGST Act, 2017 as apparatus, equipment, and machinery fixed to earth by foundation or structural support that are used for making outward supply of goods or services or both, and includes such foundation and structural supports but excludes: (i) land, building, or any other civil structures; (ii) telecommunication towers; and (iii) pipelines laid outside the factory premises. This definition is critical for Section 17(5)(c) and 17(5)(d) — the ITC block on works contract and self-construction services applies to construction of "immovable property other than plant and machinery." Thus, if the immovable property being constructed qualifies as "plant and machinery" under this Explanation, the ITC on works contract services and construction materials is not blocked. Examples where courts and AAR have examined this distinction include: blast furnaces, reactors, petroleum refinery equipment, and fixed storage tanks. The test is functional: is the apparatus/equipment "fixed to earth" for the purpose of making outward supplies? The exclusion of "land, building, and civil structures" means that even if a building is used for manufacturing, its construction does not qualify as "plant and machinery" for ITC purposes.
