# Disputed and Ambiguous Areas — RCM, Blocked ITC, and Apportionment

{topic: "Cross-Topic — Disputed Areas"}

## Overview
Advanced GST practice requires not just knowing the settled law but also identifying where the law is unclear, where CBIC guidance conflicts with statutory text, and where courts have ruled differently from the department's position. This file catalogs the most significant disputed and ambiguous areas across all three topics as of May 2026.

**Risk rating**: Each dispute is rated:
- `HIGH RISK` — Department frequently raises demand; significant exposure
- `MEDIUM RISK` — Some AAR/departmental positions against taxpayer; addressable with documentation
- `LOW RISK` — Limited audit exposure; CBIC guidance supports taxpayer in most cases

## Topic 1: RCM — Disputed Areas

### Dispute 1.1: Motor Vehicle Renting — LLP and Partnership Firms
{law_reference: "Notification 22/2017-IT(Rate) as amended; Section 9(3) CGST Act"}
{risk: "HIGH RISK"}

**The dispute**: Notification 22/2017 (motor vehicle renting under RCM) covers "body corporates." LLPs and partnership firms are not "body corporates" under the Companies Act.

**CBIC position**: No explicit clarification as of May 2026 covering LLPs.

**Department position (field level)**: Some GST officers have demanded RCM on motor vehicle renting for LLPs, arguing that the notification is taxpayer-protective (taxpayer should pay RCM and claim ITC rather than forward charge).

**Taxpayer position**: LLPs are not body corporates; notification does not apply; forward charge by supplier applies.

**Judicial/AAR position**: Mixed. Some Maharashtra AARs have sided with the taxpayer (LLP is not a body corporate). Others have applied a substance-over-form approach and held that LLPs should be treated similarly to companies.

**Practical advice**: For Maharashtra LLPs with significant motor vehicle hire expenses: obtain an advance ruling from Maharashtra AAR before taking a position.

---

### Dispute 1.2: GTA Services — 5% RCM vs 12% Forward Charge
{law_reference: "Notification 13/2017-CT(Rate) as amended; GTA Forward Charge Notification"}
{risk: "MEDIUM RISK"}

**The dispute**: GTA services received by a registered person are subject to RCM @ 5%. However, since 2022, GTAs may opt for forward charge at 12% (with ITC to the GTA). If the GTA has opted for forward charge but the recipient still tries to pay RCM (or vice versa), double taxation or no taxation may occur.

**Current position (as of May 2026)**: 
- GTA must file a declaration at the start of each year indicating whether they are on forward charge or RCM
- If GTA does not file: RCM applies (recipient pays)
- If GTA files for forward charge: GTA charges GST @ 12%; no RCM for recipient

**Common error**: GTA on forward charge issues invoices without GST; recipient assumes RCM and pays. Result: recipient pays RCM that the GTA has already collected forward. Double payment.

**Practical advice**: Confirm GTA's forward/reverse charge declaration before processing. Request the GTA's Udyam/GSTIN registration and annual declaration.

### Dispute 1.3: Legal Services — Partnership Law Firms
{law_reference: "Notification 13/2017-CT(Rate) — legal services by advocate"}
{risk: "HIGH RISK"}

**The dispute**: RCM on legal services applies when provided by an "individual advocate including a senior advocate." Partnership law firms are not individuals.

**Department position**: Partnership firms providing legal services should pay forward charge (charge GST to the client). Many law firms are unregistered and do not charge GST.

**Market reality**: Many large Indian law firms operate as partnerships. They either:
1. Treat each partner as an individual advocate (aggressive — RCM applies for each partner-client engagement)
2. Treat the firm as the supplier (forward charge, but many are unregistered, creating compliance gaps)
3. File under RCM as a matter of practical compliance (most common, though arguably incorrect)

**Judicial position**: AAR Maharashtra has held that a firm of advocates is not the same as an "individual advocate" — forward charge should apply. However, many firms continue to operate under RCM for practical compliance.

**CBIC gap**: CBIC Circular 177/09/2022 addressed this but left some ambiguity for LLP law firms vs. traditional partnership firms.

**Practical advice**: For recipients receiving legal services from partnership law firms: document the firm's position (forward or reverse charge). If the firm charges GST on invoice → forward charge; if invoice is without GST → apply RCM. Maintain the invoice as evidence.

### Dispute 1.4: Section 9(4) — Current Scope (Registered vs Unregistered)
{law_reference: "Section 9(4) CGST Act as amended; Notification 38/2017-CT and subsequent"}
{risk: "LOW RISK"}

**Background**: Section 9(4) was originally applied broadly (all unregistered supplies → RCM). It was suspended in 2018, partially reinstated in 2019 for specific categories (e.g., renting of residential dwelling, security, housekeeping).

**Current position (May 2026)**: Section 9(4) RCM applies **only** to specific categories notified by the government. Not all unregistered supplies trigger RCM.

**Current notified categories under Section 9(4)**:
- Renting of residential dwelling for commercial use
- Security services by unregistered person
- Housekeeping and related services by unregistered person
- Legal services (disputed — see 1.3 above)

**Common error**: Entities assume all purchases from unregistered persons trigger RCM. Many do not — the unregistered supplier simply does not charge GST (exempt or outside threshold). Only the notified categories trigger RCM.

### Dispute 1.5: Secondment / Deputation Arrangements
{law_reference: "CBIC Circular 196/8/2023-GST; Appellate Authority rulings"}
{risk: "HIGH RISK"}

**The dispute**: Where a company seconds employees from a foreign parent or group company, does this constitute import of services? If yes → IGST under RCM.

**CBIC position (Circular 196/8/2023)**: If the foreign company is merely facilitating the arrangement with no profit margin → NOT a service → no RCM. If the foreign company bills a management fee → service exists → RCM on management fee.

**Court positions**: Some HCs and AARs have held that even where no explicit management fee is charged, the salary reimbursement is consideration for a taxable service (manpower supply under RCM). This conflicts with Circular 196.

**Maharashtra position**: Maharashtra SGST officers have raised demands on secondment arrangements, particularly for IT sector entities where seconded employees from US/European parent companies are common. Circular 196 is the primary defense.

**Practical advice**: Wherever a secondment arrangement exists, document the arrangement carefully:
- Is the overseas entity merely facilitating (and the employee continues under the overseas payroll)? → Circular 196 defense (no service, no RCM)
- Is the overseas entity providing a manpower service and being reimbursed with a margin? → IGST under RCM applies

## Topic 2: Blocked ITC — Disputed Areas

### Dispute 2.1: Expensed vs Capitalised Renovation — Judgment Call
{law_reference: "Section 17(5)(c)/(d) + Finance Act 2021 Explanation; Ind AS 16 / AS 10"}
{risk: "HIGH RISK"}

**The dispute**: The Finance Act 2021 Explanation makes the capitalisation decision the key test. But what determines whether renovation is capitalised or expensed? The accounting standards (Ind AS 16 / AS 10) require capitalisation when the expenditure:
- Increases the future economic benefit of the asset beyond the originally assessed standard
- The cost can be measured reliably

"Routine repair" vs "improvement" is a judgment call. Two accountants can disagree.

**Department position**: Officers scrutinize the fixed asset register. If renovation is capitalised, it is blocked. Officers have challenged cases where entities expensed significant renovation to avoid the ITC block.

**Taxpayer position**: Accounting policy determines the treatment; the tax outcome follows. Entities are not required to capitalise in a tax-favorable manner.

**Risk**: If an entity expenses work that a "reasonable accountant" would capitalise (to preserve ITC), the department can argue that the accounting treatment is incorrect and demand ITC reversal.

**Practical advice**: Follow the accounting standards genuinely. Do not change capitalisation treatment solely for GST purposes. Document the decision with technical assessment from a structural engineer or valuer where material.

### Dispute 2.2: Plant and Machinery vs Civil Structure — Solar Panels, HVAC, Elevators
{law_reference: "Section 17(5)(c)/(d) exception — plant and machinery definition in Explanation to Section 17"}
{risk: "HIGH RISK"}

**The dispute**: The Finance Act 2021 Explanation's definition of "plant and machinery" excludes "land, building or any other civil structures." The boundary between a machine embedded in a structure and the structure itself is contested.

**Key disputed assets:**

| Asset | Taxpayer Argument | Department Argument | AAR/Court Status |
|-------|------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Solar panels (rooftop) | Apparatus for generating power → plant | Fixed to building → civil | Maharashtra AAAR: possible plant; Finance Act 2026 clarifies |
| HVAC system in factory | Serves production → plant and machinery | Fixed to building → civil | Split AARs; CBIC no clarity |
| Factory elevator | Serves production floors → plant | Civil structure (lift shaft, structure) → civil | Split; lift mechanism vs shaft |
| Escalators in mall | Serves retail (taxable) → plant | Civil structure | Generally blocked as civil |
| EV charging infrastructure | Equipment → plant | Built into parking structure | No CBIC guidance as of May 2026 |

**Finance Act 2026 clarification**: Solar panels for captive power generation confirmed as plant and machinery (clarified). Other items remain contested.

---

### Dispute 2.3: Vehicle — Business Use vs Personal Use (Partial Use)
{law_reference: "Section 17(5)(a) CGST Act — absolute block; no proportionate exception"}
{risk: "HIGH RISK"}

**The dispute**: Section 17(5)(a) blocks ITC on motor vehicles ≤13 seats. The block is absolute — it does not provide for partial ITC based on business use percentage.

**Taxpayer argument**: A vehicle used 80% for business and 20% personal should qualify for 80% ITC, analogous to how other jurisdictions (UK, EU) handle partial vehicle use.

**CBIC/Department position**: Section 17(5)(a) contains no proportionate exception. The block is 100% regardless of actual use pattern.

**Judicial position**: No SC/HC ruling definitively permitting partial ITC on motor vehicles. A few AAR rulings have denied partial claims.

**Practical advice**: Do not claim partial ITC on company vehicles without an advance ruling. The 100% block is the legally safe position.

---

### Dispute 2.4: Group Health Insurance — "Obligatory Under Law" Threshold
{law_reference: "Section 17(5)(b)(iii) exception — notified employer obligations"}
{risk: "MEDIUM RISK"}

**The dispute**: Group health insurance is blocked under Section 17(5)(b)(iii) unless it is a service "obligatory for an employer to provide under any law." When is health insurance "obligatory under law"?

**ESIC**: For companies under ESI Act (₹10,000/month workers in certain industries), ESIC contribution is mandatory. However, GST on ESIC contribution is not typical insurance.

**Private group medical insurance**: Not mandated under any central law as of May 2026 for most sectors. Some state-specific shop and establishment acts may require health coverage.

**Department position**: Private voluntary group health insurance = blocked. No "obligatory under law" exception applies.

**Taxpayer position (aggressive)**: The intent to provide insurance as an employer obligation should qualify; many companies provide insurance as a legal/social obligation.

**Practical advice**: Unless a specific law mandates the health insurance (sector-specific regulations, government rules for specific industries), do not claim ITC on group health insurance.

## Topic 3: Apportionment — Disputed Areas

### Dispute 3.1: What Goes in the E/F Denominator — "Total Turnover"
{law_reference: "Rule 43(1)(d); Section 17(3) CGST Act"}
{risk: "HIGH RISK"}

**The dispute**: Rule 43 says F = "total turnover in the state." Section 17(3) includes various categories in "exempt supply." The outer boundary of F is disputed for:
1. **Dividend income**: Is dividend income "outside GST scope" and therefore in F? Department says yes (dilutes ratio). Some taxpayers argue it is an investment return, not a "supply."
2. **Property sale (post-OC)**: Is a completed flat sale (post-occupancy certificate) "outside GST" or "exempt"? It affects whether it goes in E or is simply excluded from F.
3. **Statutory levies received**: PF, ESIC contribution reimbursements — are they "turnover" for F?

**CBIC position**: Circular 102/21/2019 addressed banking but left other sectors without guidance.

**Practical advice**: When in doubt, include the amount in F (as non-taxable). Excluding it from F inflates the taxable ratio and increases ITC — this is the aggressive position that the department challenges. Including it in F is the conservative, lower-risk approach.

---

### Dispute 3.2: Provisional Monthly Ratio — Timing of Interest
{law_reference: "Section 50 CGST Act; Rule 43 — provisional/annual calculation"}
{risk: "MEDIUM RISK"}

**The dispute**: Where monthly provisional Te is lower than annual actual Te (i.e., entity over-claimed ITC during the year and corrects it in GSTR-9), does interest under Section 50 run from:
(a) The month of each under-reversal (each of the 12 months), or
(b) The date of GSTR-9 filing?

**Finance Act 2025 clarification**: Interest runs from the month of under-reversal (position (a)), not from GSTR-9 filing. This increases the interest burden for entities that discover significant ratios mid-year or at GSTR-9 stage.

**Practical advice**: Where the annual actual ratio is likely to differ materially from monthly provisional, switch to actual monthly calculation mid-year rather than waiting for the annual true-up. This minimizes interest exposure.

---

### Dispute 3.3: Activity-Based Attribution vs Turnover Ratio
{law_reference: "Rule 43(1)(d) — E/F formula; Section 17(2)"}
{risk: "MEDIUM RISK"}

**The dispute**: Rule 43 prescribes the E/F turnover ratio. Can a taxpayer use an alternative activity-based allocation (headcount, floor space, machine hours) if it produces a more accurate result?

**CBIC position**: Rule 43 prescribes the E/F formula as the method. There is no explicit provision for alternative methods.

**Taxpayer argument**: The statute says ITC should be "attributable to taxable supplies" (Section 17(2)) — the more accurate the attribution, the more compliant the approach.

**Department position**: Rule 43's E/F formula is the prescribed method. Alternative methods require advance approval or a specific CBIC circular.

**Court positions**: A few AARs have accepted activity-based methods as supplementary evidence, but the E/F formula remains the regulatory standard.

**Practical advice**: Always compute and report the Rule 43 E/F-based Te in GSTR-3B Table 4B(1). If activity-based allocation produces a higher ITC (higher eligible %), document it robustly and consider seeking an advance ruling before using it.

---

### Dispute 3.4: Capital Goods Changing Use — Retrospective Adjustment
{law_reference: "Rule 43 — capital goods; Section 18(6)"}
{risk: "MEDIUM RISK"}

**The dispute**: If a capital good originally classified as T1 (100% taxable) is later used partly for exempt activities, does Rule 43 apply retrospectively to the months where it was claimed as T1?

**Taxpayer position**: Rule 43 applies from the month of change in use onwards. The T1 classification in prior months was correct based on use at that time.

**Department position**: Some officers have demanded reversal for the entire period from purchase date, arguing the 60-month method should have been applied from the start.

**Judicial position**: AARs have generally supported the prospective change approach — reversal from the month of actual change in use. No SC ruling as of May 2026.

**Practical advice**: Document the date of change in use carefully (board resolution, management memo). Reverse prospectively from that date. Maintain evidence that the asset was exclusively for taxable use prior to that date.

## **[MAHARASHTRA-SPECIFIC]** Maharashtra Exclusive Disputes

### Maharashtra Dispute M.1: Commercial Common Area Electricity
{law_reference: "CBIC Circular 206/18/2023 (residential complexes) vs Maharashtra SGST officer position"}
{risk: "HIGH RISK for Maharashtra entities"}

CBIC Circular 206/18/2023 addresses common area electricity for **residential** complexes. Maharashtra SGST officers have extrapolated this to commercial buildings.

**CBIC position**: Residential complex common area electricity → blocked.

**Maharashtra SGST officer position**: Commercial building common area electricity → also blocked.

**Legal basis for Maharashtra position**: Weak — the circular is explicitly for residential complexes. CBIC has not extended this to commercial buildings.

**Recommended response to Maharashtra notices**: Cite the circular's limited scope (residential only) and argue that commercial building common area electricity is T3 (apportionable) not blocked.

---

### Maharashtra Dispute M.2: Inter-Company Interest
{law_reference: "Section 17(3) CGST Act — exempt supply; Rule 43"}
{risk: "MEDIUM RISK"}

Maharashtra SGST officers have in some cases demanded that interest income from inter-company loans to related parties be included in E (exempt supply denominator), regardless of whether this is the CBIC position.

**CBIC position (Circular 102)**: Interest income from loans is exempt supply → included in E. This applies to banking sector primarily.

**Maharashtra position**: Extends to all inter-company loans regardless of business activity.

**For non-banking companies**: The CBIC circular 102 was addressed to banking companies. Applying it to a manufacturing company that occasionally makes inter-company loans is an aggressive departmental position. However, Section 17(3) does include "supplies on which the recipient is liable to pay tax on reverse charge basis" — interest exempt from GST still falls within "exempt supply" broadly.

**Practical advice**: For Maharashtra entities with significant inter-company lending: include the interest income in E (conservative, avoids dispute) OR obtain an advance ruling from Maharashtra AAR.
