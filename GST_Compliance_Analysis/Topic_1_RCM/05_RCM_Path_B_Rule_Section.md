## How to Use Path B

Path B organises the Reverse Charge Mechanism (RCM) framework by its **legislative and regulatory architecture** — working from the statute (CGST Act, IGST Act) through to the rules (CGST Rules, 2017) and finally to the notifications and circulars that operationalise each provision. This is the *scholar's* and *litigator's* path.

**When to use Path B:**

1. **Advance Rulings (AAR) and appellate proceedings:** When arguing a position before the Authority for Advance Rulings (AAR) or the Appellate Authority for Advance Rulings (AAAR), the legal basis of RCM must be traced through the Rule and Section. AAR orders are routinely tested on whether the specific Rule entry covers the transaction. Path B provides that framework.

2. **Audit defence and assessment proceedings:** When responding to a Show Cause Notice (SCN) or during a departmental audit under Section 65/66 CGST Act, the departmental officer's allegation will cite a specific Rule/Section/Notification. Path B enables a structured response tracing whether each element of the alleged liability is made out.

3. **Academic and training purposes:** CA, CS, and CMA exam preparation requires a clear understanding of the statutory scheme before moving to practical application. Path B provides the foundation.

4. **Compliance system design:** When a company designs its ERP/SAP system for automatic RCM determination, it needs the rule-by-rule, section-by-section logic tree that Path B provides.

5. **Cross-border / IGST research:** Import of services, place of supply research, and OIDAR compliance require deep engagement with the IGST Act provisions covered in Path B.

**Companion document:** Path A (File 04) is the practitioner's transaction-first reference. Path B and Path A should be used together — Path A to identify the transaction type; Path B to understand the rule-by-rule mechanics.

**Structure of Path B:**

This document is organised in the following order:
1. Rule 3 CGST Rules — the primary rule specifying which supplies attract RCM.
2. Rule 4 CGST Rules — exclusions and exemptions from RCM.

## Rule 3 CGST Rules — Supplies Subject to RCM
{law_reference: "Rule 3 CGST Rules, 2017; Section 9(3) CGST Act, 2017; Section 5(3) IGST Act, 2017; Notification No. 13/2017-CT(Rate) dated 28-Jun-2017; Notification No. 10/2017-IGST(Rate)"}

### Statutory Text of Rule 3

Rule 3 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Rules, 2017 (as notified and amended) reads as follows:

> **Rule 3 — Supplies Covered Under Reverse Charge**
>
> *(1)* The supply of goods or services or both, on which the tax is payable on reverse charge basis under sub-section (3) of section 9 of the CGST Act and section 5(3) of the IGST Act shall be determined on the basis of the categories of supply of goods or services or both notified by the Government on the recommendation of the Council.
>
> *(2)* The Government may, on the recommendation of the Council, by notification, notify the categories of supply of goods or services or both on which the tax shall be paid on reverse charge basis by the recipient of such goods or services or both and all the provisions of the Act shall apply to such recipient as if he is the person liable for paying the tax in relation to the supply of such goods or services or both.

**Important contextual note:** The rule text, read in isolation, is skeletal — it delegates the identification of the actual categories to the Government's notification power. Rule 3 itself does not list the specific supplies; it establishes the legal framework within which the notifications operate. The substantive list of RCM supplies is found in **Notification No. 13/2017-CT(Rate)** (for CGST and SGST) and **Notification No. 10/2017-IGST(Rate)** (for IGST on inter-state supplies, including import of services), both dated 28 June 2017 and amended extensively thereafter.

### Line-by-Line Annotation

**Sub-rule (1):**

*"The supply of goods or services or both, on which the tax is payable on reverse charge basis under sub-section (3) of section 9 of the CGST Act and section 5(3) of the IGST Act shall be determined on the basis of the categories of supply of goods or services or both notified by the Government on the recommendation of the Council."*

**Annotation:**
- "determined on the basis of... categories... notified": The rule explicitly makes the scope of RCM under Section 9(3)/5(3) dependent on notifications. Without a notification, no RCM arises under this sub-rule even if a supply intuitively "should" be on RCM.
- "on the recommendation of the Council": The GST Council's recommendation is a procedural prerequisite. Notifications issued without Council recommendation are legally vulnerable. However, courts have generally upheld notifications where they are traceable to Council decisions recorded in meeting minutes, even where formal recommendation documentation may be imprecise.
- "goods or services or both": All three types of supply are covered. RCM is not limited to services — it can apply to goods as well (though in practice, the Section 9(3) notifications primarily cover services).
- "Section 9(3) CGST Act and Section 5(3) IGST Act": The dual citation is important. Section 9(3) CGST covers intra-state supplies. Section 5(3) IGST covers inter-state supplies (including import of services). Both operate in parallel and the notifications under each must be read together.

**Sub-rule (2):**

*"The Government may, on the recommendation of the Council, by notification, notify the categories of supply of goods or services or both on which the tax shall be paid on reverse charge basis by the recipient..."*

**Annotation:**
- "tax shall be paid... by the recipient": This sub-rule confirms the fundamental principle — the **recipient** (not the supplier) is the taxpayer. All provisions of the CGST Act that would otherwise apply to the supplier as the taxpayer now apply to the recipient.
- "as if he is the person liable for paying the tax": This deeming fiction is crucial. It means:
  - The recipient must comply with registration requirements even if their own turnover is below the threshold (Section 24(iii) CGST Act — compulsory registration for persons liable to pay tax under RCM).
  - The recipient must issue a self-invoice (Section 31(3)(f)).
  - The recipient must file the relevant returns.
  - All penalties, interest, and recovery provisions apply to the recipient.

### Is Rule 3 Exhaustive?

**Short answer: The Rule itself is not a list; the notifications issued under it are exhaustive as to Section 9(3) RCM.**

**Detailed analysis:**

Rule 3 is a framework rule — it delegates the task of specifying the actual categories to the notification power. The notifications issued under Rule 3 read with Section 9(3) — primarily Notification 13/2017-CT(Rate) and its amendments — are **exhaustive** as to which services attract RCM under the Section 9(3) route. If a service does not appear in the notification, it does not attract Section 9(3) RCM, regardless of how it might appear that the supply should attract reverse charge.

**This exhaustive principle has been upheld by multiple AAR decisions and High Court observations:**
- The AAR in several states has held that RCM cannot be extended by analogy to services similar to but not listed in the notification.
- The Madras High Court (in the context of a taxpayer seeking to avoid forward charge by claiming RCM should apply) has held that RCM applies only when specifically notified, and the notification must be strictly construed.

**However:** Note that Section 9(4) operates on a *different basis* — it applies to notified categories of supplies from *unregistered* suppliers, and the relevant Notification 7/2019-CT(Rate) specifies those categories. This is not covered by Rule 3's framework (which implements Section 9(3)); Section 9(4) operates through its own notification power. See the Rule 4 section for the interaction between Section 9(3) and 9(4).

**Practical implication:** A taxpayer cannot be compelled to pay RCM on a supply that is not in Notification 13/2017-CT(Rate) or its amendments, even if the department argues the supply is "similar" to a notified category. The notification is both enabling and limiting.

### Interaction with Section 9(3) and Section 9(4)

**Section 9(3) — the "what is notified" route:**
Rule 3 implements Section 9(3). The Government notifies specific *categories* of supply (not supplier-registration-status-based). Whether the supplier is registered or unregistered is irrelevant for Section 9(3) — if the supply falls in the notification, RCM applies.

**Section 9(4) — the "unregistered supplier" route:**
Section 9(4) operates entirely separately from Rule 3. It is not implemented through Rule 3; it has its own notification power and its own notification (Notification 7/2019-CT(Rate)). Section 9(4) is concerned with the *registration status* of the supplier, not the category of supply per se. Rule 3 does NOT cover Section 9(4) categories — practitioners must check both Rule 3 (Section 9(3) categories) and Notification 7/2019-CT(Rate) (Section 9(4) categories) independently for any given transaction.

**Double-coverage:** It is possible (in theory) for a transaction to be covered by both Section 9(3) and Section 9(4). For example, if an unregistered person provides security services to a registered non-body-corporate recipient: Section 9(3) (via Notification 13/2017 Entry 14, which covers non-body-corporate security suppliers) covers it. Section 9(4) might also cover it if security services are notified under Section 9(4). In practice, Section 9(3) notification coverage takes precedence and is the primary applicable provision.

## Rule 4 CGST Rules — Exemptions and Exclusions
{law_reference: "Rule 4 CGST Rules, 2017; Section 11 CGST Act, 2017; Section 10 CGST Act, 2017 (Composition); Notification No. 12/2017-CT(Rate) (Exempt Services); Notification No. 2/2017-CT(Rate) (Exempt Goods)"}

### Statutory Text of Rule 4

Rule 4 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Rules, 2017 reads as follows:

> **Rule 4 — Reverse Charge — Exemptions**
>
> *(1)* Where a registered person is required to pay tax under reverse charge in respect of a supply received by him, but the supply is exempt from tax, no tax shall be payable on reverse charge.
>
> *(2)* Where a registered person who opts to pay tax under section 10 (i.e., the Composition Levy) receives a supply on which tax is payable under reverse charge, he shall pay the applicable tax on reverse charge but shall not be entitled to take credit of the said tax.
>
> *(3)* The provisions of sub-section (3) of section 9 shall not apply to any service supplied by any person to the Government of India or a State Government or a Union Territory.*

*Note: The exact legislative language of the CGST Rules may be updated by delegated legislation; the above represents the substantive position as of May 2026.*

### Line-by-Line Annotation

**Sub-rule (1) — Exempt supply: no RCM:**

*"Where... the supply is exempt from tax, no tax shall be payable on reverse charge."*

**Annotation:**

This is a logical and fundamental rule: the RCM is a mechanism for collecting GST on a taxable supply. If the underlying supply is itself exempt (either under Section 11 of the CGST Act via the exemption notifications, or because it falls outside the scope of supply entirely), then there is no GST to collect, and the RCM mechanism does not impose any liability.

**Critical nuance — exempt vs zero-rated:**
- **Exempt supplies:** GST rate = 0% (explicitly exempted by notification). No ITC on inputs for exempt supplies (Section 17(2)). RCM does not apply.
- **Zero-rated supplies:** GST rate = 0% but eligible for ITC refund (export of goods/services). These are NOT "exempt" in the same sense — GST is technically applicable at 0%. However, in practice, the RCM question for zero-rated supplies is moot since the rate is 0%.
- **Nil-rated supplies:** Rate = 0% in the rate schedule. Similar position to exempt for most purposes.

**Practical application:**
- If a GTA transports goods which are exempt from GST (e.g., agricultural produce), the GTA's transportation service itself may still attract GST (transportation of agricultural produce is now generally taxable in most circumstances). However, if the transportation service is specifically exempt (e.g., transportation of agriculture produce by road where the service itself is exempt under Notification 12/2017-CT(Rate)), then no RCM.
- Legal services to a private individual (not a business entity): no GST applies (recipient not a business entity); effectively exempt from RCM.

**Sub-rule (2) — Composition dealer: pays RCM but no ITC:**

*"Where a registered person who opts to pay tax under section 10... receives a supply on which tax is payable under reverse charge, he shall pay the applicable tax on reverse charge but shall not be entitled to take credit of the said tax."*

**Annotation:**

This sub-rule deals with the interaction between the **Composition Scheme (Section 10)** and RCM:

- A composition dealer opts for a simplified tax compliance regime: they pay a flat rate on their turnover and are not required to charge GST on their outward supplies.
- However, when a composition dealer RECEIVES a supply that triggers RCM (e.g., GTA services for their business, legal services), they are STILL required to pay the RCM GST on that inward supply.
- **But** — and this is the critical disqualification — the composition dealer **cannot claim ITC** on the RCM tax paid. This is consistent with the overall composition scheme design: composition dealers have no ITC entitlement.

**Why does this matter in practice?**
- A small manufacturer under composition scheme (say, a small food products maker in Maharashtra paying composition tax at 1% on turnover) who uses GTA for delivering goods must pay 5% RCM on freight. This 5% cannot be recovered as ITC. It is a pure cost.
- This is one of the factors that makes the composition scheme less attractive for businesses with significant inward supplies that would attract RCM.

**[MAHARASHTRA-SPECIFIC]** Maharashtra has a significant population of composition dealers in the MSME sector (small manufacturers, traders). The Maharashtra GST department has identified composition dealers failing to pay RCM on GTA services as a compliance gap, and has included this in its audit scope for MSME assessees.

**Sub-rule (3) — Government recipient exclusion:**

*"The provisions of sub-section (3) of section 9 shall not apply to any service supplied by any person to the Government of India or a State Government or a Union Territory."*

**Annotation:**

Where the **recipient** of services is the Central Government, State Government, or Union Territory administration, Section 9(3) RCM does not apply. The rationale is that:
- Government departments are sovereign; they may not be GST registered in all cases.
- Compliance burden on government departments for RCM payments would be practically difficult.
- Government ultimately bears the cost of GST on the consolidated fund side, creating a circular flow.

**However, note:** This exclusion applies to Section 9(3). Government bodies that ARE registered under GST (e.g., PSUs, government companies) may still be subject to RCM as registered recipients. The exclusion is specifically for the government as sovereign/constitutional entity, not for government-owned commercial entities.

### Composition Dealer Exclusion

The composition dealer exclusion deserves additional elaboration given its practical importance:

**What the composition dealer must do (RCM-related):**
1. Pay RCM in cash on any supplies received that are notified under Section 9(3) or 9(4).
2. File **GSTR-4** (annual return for composition dealers), where inward supplies on which RCM is paid must be disclosed.
3. Also file the CMP-08 quarterly statement which includes self-assessed tax (including RCM tax).
4. Issue a **self-invoice** for each RCM transaction.

**What the composition dealer CANNOT do:**
- Claim any ITC on the RCM tax paid (Section 10(4) expressly bars ITC for composition taxpayers).
- Pass on any credit to downstream buyers (since composition dealers don't collect GST).

**RCM effectively a pure cost for composition dealers:** Since neither forward ITC nor RCM ITC is available, every RCM payment is a dead cost for the composition dealer. For example, a composition dealer using a GTA for ₹1,00,000 freight must pay ₹5,000 RCM — which is an actual tax cost since no ITC can be claimed.

### Exempted Supply Exclusion

When the underlying supply (the goods or services received) is itself exempt from GST, no RCM liability arises. The following framework governs:

**Supplies exempt from GST (illustrative):**
- Health care services by a clinical establishment (exempt under Notification 12/2017-CT(Rate)).
- Educational services provided by educational institutions (exempt).
- Agricultural produce in certain forms (exempt from rate schedule).
- Transportation of agricultural produce by non-GTA transport (specifically, transport of agriculture produce by road by a person other than GTA — exempt).

**Effect:** If a service falls entirely within the exempt category in Notification 12/2017-CT(Rate) or goods fall within Notification 2/2017-CT(Rate), no RCM liability arises even if the supplier-recipient combination would otherwise trigger RCM.

**Cautionary note:** Partial exemptions require apportionment. If a supply is partly taxable and partly exempt, only the taxable portion attracts RCM.

### Threshold-Based Exclusion

**Current position (May 2026):** There is **no aggregate turnover threshold** for RCM liability. A recipient is liable to pay RCM from the first rupee of a notified supply received, regardless of the total quantum of RCM supplies in the year.

**Historical position:** Under the original Section 9(4) as enacted in 2017 (before the 2018 amendment), the blanket reverse charge on all unregistered supplier purchases was unsustainable. The government initially exempted supplies below ₹5,000 per day per supplier (aggregate). However, this was in the context of the old blanket Section 9(4) which has since been restructured. No such threshold currently applies under either Section 9(3) or the post-2019 Section 9(4) framework.

**Compulsory registration consequence:** Under Section 24(iii) of the CGST Act, any person who is required to pay tax under the reverse charge mechanism is compulsorily required to register, regardless of their aggregate turnover. This means even a start-up or small business with zero turnover must register if they receive notified RCM supplies. However, if all supplies received are from registered suppliers and do not fall under Section 9(3) notified categories, and the recipient's own outward turnover is below the threshold, registration may not be required.

## Rule 5 CGST Rules — Payment Timing and Recovery
{law_reference: "Rule 5 CGST Rules, 2017; Section 12(3) CGST Act (time of supply for goods-RCM); Section 13(3) CGST Act (time of supply for services-RCM); Section 50 CGST Act (interest); Section 79 CGST Act (recovery)"}

### Statutory Text of Rule 5

Rule 5 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Rules, 2017 reads as follows:

> **Rule 5 — Time of Supply in Case of Reverse Charge**
>
> *(1)* The time of supply of goods, where the tax is payable on reverse charge basis, shall be the earliest of:
> *(a)* the date of receipt of goods; or
> *(b)* the date of payment as entered in the books of account of the recipient or the date on which the payment is debited in his bank account, whichever is earlier; or
> *(c)* the date immediately following thirty days from the date of issue of invoice or any other document, by whatever name called, in lieu thereof by the supplier.
>
> Provided that where it is not possible to determine the time of supply under clause (a), (b), or (c), the time of supply shall be the date of entry in the books of account of the recipient of supply.
>
> *(2)* The time of supply of services, where the tax is payable on reverse charge basis, shall be the earliest of:
> *(a)* the date of payment as entered in the books of account of the recipient or the date on which the payment is debited in his bank account, whichever is earlier; or
> *(b)* the date immediately following sixty days from the date of issue of invoice or any other document, by whatever name called, in lieu thereof by the supplier.
>
> Provided that where it is not possible to determine the time of supply under clause (a) or (b), the time of supply shall be the date of entry in the books of account of the recipient of supply.
>
> Provided further that in case of supply by associated enterprises, where the supplier of service is located outside India, the time of supply shall be the date of entry in the books of account of the recipient or the date of payment, whichever is earlier.

### Time of Supply for RCM

Understanding the time of supply is critical because it determines:
- When the GST liability arises.
- Which tax period's GSTR-3B must include the payment.
- From when interest under Section 50 runs if payment is delayed.

**For goods on RCM — three triggers, earliest wins:**

| Trigger | Date |
|---|---|
| (a) Date of receipt of goods | Physical date of delivery to recipient |
| (b) Date of payment in books or bank debit | Whichever is earlier between book entry and bank debit |
| (c) 30th day from supplier's invoice | If goods not received and payment not made within 30 days of invoice |

**For services on RCM — two triggers, earliest wins:**

| Trigger | Date |
|---|---|
| (a) Date of payment in books or bank debit | Whichever is earlier between book entry and bank debit |
| (b) 61st day from supplier's invoice | Date immediately following 60 days from supplier's invoice date |

**Critical difference: goods vs services:**
- For goods: 30-day rule from invoice.
- For services: 60-day rule from invoice.
- For goods: receipt of goods is also a trigger (not so for services).

**Special rule for associated enterprises (import of services):**
Where a foreign associated enterprise (parent company, group company) provides services to the Indian entity (subsidiary/branch), the time of supply is the **date of entry in books of account** or the **date of payment**, whichever is earlier. The 60-day invoice rule does not apply to associated enterprise transactions. This is an anti-avoidance provision — it prevents associated enterprises from delaying invoicing to defer RCM liability.

**Practical example — time of supply calculation:**

> Facts: Indian company ABC Ltd. receives legal services from UK law firm on 1 February 2026. UK firm issues invoice on 5 February 2026. ABC Ltd. pays the fee on 20 March 2026 (bank debit on 20 March 2026). ABC Ltd. enters the provision in books on 28 February 2026.
>
> Time of supply calculation for RCM (services):
> - (a) Date of payment in books = 28 February 2026; bank debit = 20 March 2026 → Earlier = 28 February 2026.
> - (b) 61st day from 5 February = 7 April 2026.
> - Earliest = **28 February 2026.**
>
> GST liability arises on 28 February 2026. Must be in February 2026's GSTR-3B (due 20 March 2026). If not included, interest runs from 21 March 2026 at 18% p.a.

### Cash Ledger Only for RCM Payment

This is one of the most practically significant and frequently misunderstood aspects of RCM compliance:

**The rule:** RCM GST liability **must be paid using the Electronic Cash Ledger only.** The Electronic Credit Ledger (ITC balance) **cannot** be used to discharge RCM liability.

**Legal basis:**
- Section 49(4) of the CGST Act: "The amount available in the electronic credit ledger may be used for making any payment towards output tax under this Act or under the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act in such manner and subject to such conditions and within such time as may be prescribed."
- **Output tax definition — Section 2(82):** "Output tax in relation to a taxable person, means the tax chargeable under this Act on taxable supply of goods or services or both made by him or by his agent."
- **RCM tax is output tax of the recipient:** When a recipient pays RCM, they are the "supplier" by deeming fiction for that supply. The RCM tax becomes their output tax.
- **However**, Section 49(4) read with the Rules restricts use of ITC for output tax only in the "normal" sense. For RCM specifically, the GST Council's policy (and the implementation in the portal) requires cash payment for RCM.
- **Rule 86(2) CGST Rules:** "The balance in the electronic credit ledger referred to in sub-rule (1) may be used for making any payment towards output tax under the provisions of the Act but shall not be used for making payment of tax payable under sub-section (3) or sub-section (4) of section 9."

**Rule 86(2) CGST Rules** is the explicit rule barring use of the Electronic Credit Ledger for RCM (Section 9(3) and 9(4)) payments. This rule is unambiguous.

**Practical implication — the cash flow requirement:**

A company with a large ITC balance in its credit ledger cannot use that ITC to pay RCM. It must make actual cash outflow to the government for RCM, even though it will recoup that cash through ITC credit in the next step. For large companies with significant RCM obligations (e.g., a manufacturing company paying ₹10 crore in GTA freight and getting 5% RCM = ₹50 lakh), this creates a monthly cash outflow for RCM that is then recovered as ITC — a working capital cycle that requires planning.

**The ITC credit cycle for RCM:**
```
Step 1: Pay RCM in cash (₹X) → funds flow from Bank Account → Electronic Cash Ledger → Government
Step 2: Self-invoice issued; GSTR-3B Table 3.1(d) shows RCM liability
Step 3: ITC of ₹X credited to Electronic Credit Ledger in the SAME month (or later)
Step 4: The ITC (₹X) can now be used to offset future outward GST liability
```

For businesses making primarily taxable outward supplies, the net annual cost of RCM = zero (since ITC offsets it). The cost is only the **time value of money / working capital** during the period between cash payment of RCM and utilisation of the ITC credit.

**Exceptions — blocked ITC:** If the RCM supply falls under Section 17(5) (blocked ITC), the ITC generated from RCM payment cannot be claimed. In that case, the RCM is a genuine tax cost. Example: RCM on motor vehicle renting for personal use of employees — if blocked under Section 17(5)(b), the RCM paid becomes a cost.

### Recovery from Supplier

**Can the recipient recover RCM tax paid from the supplier?**

**Legal position:**

GST law places the liability for RCM payment on the **recipient** as a matter of statute. The law creates no obligation on the supplier to reimburse the recipient for RCM paid. The supplier neither charges GST nor participates in the payment.

However, parties are free to negotiate contractual terms. In practice, the commercial arrangement may provide for one of the following:

1. **Price adjustment:** The supplier agrees to reduce their fee/price by the GST equivalent, knowing the buyer will pay RCM. (Price = ₹100 net of tax; buyer pays ₹100 to supplier + 18% GST = ₹18 to government = total cost ₹118 = same as if supplier charged ₹118 with GST.)

2. **Gross-up clause:** The agreement provides that the stated fee is "exclusive of all taxes" and the buyer bears all applicable taxes. In this case, the RCM is entirely a buyer cost and there is no recovery from supplier.

3. **Contractual recovery clause:** Some sophisticated agreements (especially in the B2B space) include a clause allowing the buyer to recover RCM from the supplier if the RCM obligation arises from the supplier's failure to register or comply with GST. Courts have not uniformly upheld such clauses; enforcement is fact-specific.

**Court positions:**

Indian courts have not definitively ruled on the right to contractual recovery of RCM from suppliers in most contexts. The general principle of **privity of contract** allows parties to agree on economic allocation of tax burdens. However, where the RCM is a statutory liability of the recipient, the government has no concern with the inter-party recovery — it looks solely to the recipient for payment.

The **Calcutta High Court** (in a pre-GST service tax context) observed that statutory tax liabilities that fall on one party cannot be passed on to another party simply by contract, if the statute is clear on who the taxpayer is. By extension, the recipient cannot escape RCM liability by arguing the supplier should pay — but the recipient may have a separate private right of action against the supplier if the contract so provides.

**[MAHARASHTRA-SPECIFIC]** The Bombay High Court has not (as of May 2026) issued a definitive ruling on contractual recovery of RCM from suppliers specifically in the GST context, though several petitions on related issues have been filed. Maharashtra-based companies in construction and manufacturing sectors that deal with large volumes of unregistered/small suppliers frequently attempt to negotiate price-minus-tax arrangements. Legal practitioners in Maharashtra advise their corporate clients to include explicit tax responsibility clauses in vendor contracts to manage this commercially.

### Interest for Late Payment

**Section 50 of the CGST Act, 2017 — interest on delayed payment of tax:**

> *(1) Every person who is liable to pay tax in accordance with the provisions of this Act or the rules made thereunder, but fails to pay the tax or any part thereof to the Government within the period prescribed shall for the period for which the tax or any part thereof remains unpaid, pay, on his own, interest at such rate, not exceeding eighteen per cent., as may be notified by the Government on the recommendations of the Council.*
>
> *(3) Where the input tax credit has been wrongly availed and utilised, the taxable person shall pay interest on such input tax credit wrongly availed and utilised, at such rate not exceeding twenty-four per cent, as may be notified by the Government on the recommendations of the Council.*

**Applicable rate for late RCM payment:** 18% per annum on the unpaid RCM amount.

**Notification specifying the rate:** The Government has notified the rate of interest at:
- 18% per annum for late payment of tax (including RCM) under Section 50(1).
- 24% per annum for wrongly availed and utilised ITC under Section 50(3).

**Calculation methodology — simple interest:**

Interest under Section 50 is calculated on a **simple interest** basis (not compound interest) for the period of delay. The formula:

```
Interest = (Tax Amount × Rate × Number of Days of Delay) / 365
```

Where the number of days of delay is counted from the **due date of filing the return** (the date by which tax should have been paid) to the **actual date of payment**.

**Supreme Court judgment on interest — Union of India v. Bharti Airtel Ltd. and related matters:**
The Supreme Court has addressed the interest computation under Section 50 in several telecom/corporate tax contexts. The broader principle affirmed is that interest under tax statutes is compensatory (not punitive) and accrues from the date of default regardless of the assessee's intent. For RCM specifically, if the recipient fails to include RCM liability in GSTR-3B and pay it in cash for the relevant month, interest runs from the due date of that return.

**Finance Act 2021 amendment to Section 50(1):**
A significant amendment (retrospectively effective from 1 July 2017) provides that interest for late payment of tax **under Section 50(1)** is payable **on the net cash tax liability** (i.e., tax paid through cash ledger only), not on the gross tax liability. This means if a taxpayer has ITC balance but delays using it to offset outward tax, interest runs only on the actual cash shortfall. However, for **RCM** — which is always a cash payment obligation — this amendment does not change the position significantly (since RCM is entirely a cash liability, any delay means the full RCM amount is the interest base).

**Period of delay — practical calculation:**

For GSTR-3B filed by 20th of the following month (for monthly filers):
- RCM liability for March 2026 should be paid by 20 April 2026.
- If paid on 15 May 2026 (25 days late): Interest = Tax × 18% × 25/365.
- On ₹1,00,000 RCM: Interest = ₹1,00,000 × 18% × 25/365 = ₹1,233 approximately.

**Interest for periods of undetected non-compliance:**
Where RCM was not paid for multiple years (e.g., company was unaware of RCM obligation on director's sitting fees for 3 years), the interest can become significant:
- ₹50,000 per month sitting fees; RCM @ 18% = ₹9,000 per month.
- 36 months of non-compliance = ₹3,24,000 RCM principal.
- Interest at 18% p.a. for 36 months average = approximately ₹58,320 to ₹87,480 depending on when each month's liability is treated as due.
- Plus penalties under Section 74 (if suppression/fraud alleged) — up to 100% of tax.

## Rule 36(3) — ITC Eligibility on Self-Invoice
{law_reference: "Rule 36(3) CGST Rules, 2017; Section 16 CGST Act, 2017; Section 17(5) CGST Act, 2017"}

### ITC on RCM Self-Invoice

**Rule 36(3) of the CGST Rules, 2017** governs the conditions under which input tax credit can be availed on the basis of a self-invoice issued under Section 31(3)(f):

> *"Input tax credit shall be availed by a registered person in respect of inward supplies on which tax is payable on reverse charge basis, whether or not the tax has been paid, but subject to the payment of tax by him under sub-section (3) or (4) of section 9 of the CGST Act."*

**Interpretation:** While the rule text says "whether or not the tax has been paid," there is a fundamental condition: ITC can only be effectively utilised after the tax is actually paid. This is because:

1. The ITC on RCM appears in the Electronic Credit Ledger only when it is claimed in GSTR-3B Table 4.
2. The tax payment through the Electronic Cash Ledger must have occurred (or must occur in the same period) for the claim to be legally sustainable.
3. Section 16(2)(c) of the CGST Act (as amended by the Finance Act 2021, effective 1 October 2022): "the tax charged in respect of such supply has been actually paid to the Government, either in cash or through utilization of input tax credit admissible in respect of the said supply." For RCM, this means the RCM cash payment is a prerequisite for ITC.

**The sequence in practice:**
1. Issue self-invoice.
2. Pay RCM in cash (Electronic Cash Ledger) in GSTR-3B.
3. In the same GSTR-3B (Table 4A(3)) or any subsequent GSTR-3B: claim ITC.
4. ITC appears in Electronic Credit Ledger.
5. Use ITC to offset future outward GST liability.

**Can ITC be claimed in the same period as payment?** Yes. Most practically, the RCM payment and ITC claim appear in the same GSTR-3B return — the cash paid flows out for RCM (Table 3.1(d)) and the ITC is simultaneously credited (Table 4). For compliant businesses, the net effect within a period is:
- Cash outflow: RCM payment.
- Credit inflow: RCM ITC.
- Net effect in the period: Zero (if fully ITC-eligible) — just a circular flow through the government's ledger.

### Documentation Requirement

**Rule 36(3) conditions:**
1. The document must be a **self-invoice** validly issued under Section 31(3)(f) read with Rule 46.
2. The self-invoice must contain all mandatory fields (see Rule 46 section below).
3. The supply must be used **in the course or furtherance of business** (Section 16(1)).
4. The recipient must not have received any document (tax invoice) from the supplier — the self-invoice is issued precisely because the supplier (being unregistered or being a foreign supplier) cannot issue a CGST-compliant invoice.

**Section 16(2) conditions still apply:**
Even with a valid self-invoice and cash payment of RCM, the ITC claim must satisfy Section 16(2) requirements:
- *(a)* The self-invoice is in possession of the recipient — YES (he issued it himself).
- *(b)* The recipient has received the supply — must be verified.
- *(c)* The tax has been actually paid to the government — YES (cash payment through ECL).
- *(d)* The recipient has furnished a return — must be in compliance with return filing.

**Section 17(5) — blocked ITC must be checked:**
Even if all Section 16(2) conditions are met, if the RCM supply falls under Section 17(5) (blocked credits), ITC cannot be claimed. Major Section 17(5) categories relevant to RCM:
- Motor vehicles for personal use: blocked.
- Food/beverages: blocked.
- Outdoor catering: blocked.
- Club membership fees: blocked.
- Health services for employees: blocked.
- Works contract services for construction of immovable property: blocked.
- Goods/services for personal consumption: blocked.

## Rule 46 — Self-Invoice Issuance
{law_reference: "Rule 46 CGST Rules, 2017; Section 31(3)(f) CGST Act, 2017; Section 31(3)(g) CGST Act, 2017"}

### When Self-Invoice is Required

**Section 31(3)(f) of the CGST Act, 2017** mandates:
> *"A registered person who is liable to pay tax under sub-section (3) or sub-section (4) of section 9 shall issue an invoice in respect of goods or services or both received by him from the supplier who is not registered on the date of receipt of goods or services or both."*

**Scenarios requiring self-invoice (comprehensive list):**

1. **Unregistered supplier — Section 9(3) notified supply:** When an unregistered person provides a notified service (e.g., an individual advocate providing legal services to a business entity), the business entity recipient must issue a self-invoice.

2. **Unregistered supplier — Section 9(4) notified category:** When an unregistered supplier provides a Section 9(4)-notified supply to a registered person, the registered person issues a self-invoice.

3. **Import of services:** When a registered person in India receives services from a foreign supplier (outside India), the Indian recipient issues a self-invoice. The foreign supplier may or may not issue their own invoice; the self-invoice is the Indian GST-compliant document.

4. **Associated enterprise services from outside India:** Same as import of services; self-invoice required.

5. **Government services where government does not issue a GST invoice:** When a registered business entity receives services from the Central/State Government (e.g., spectrum allocation, mining lease) and the government body does not issue a GST-compliant invoice, the business entity issues a self-invoice.

**Scenario where self-invoice may NOT be required:**
If the unregistered supplier issues their own invoice (a commercial invoice, even if not a GST tax invoice), the registered recipient may use that as the supporting document internally, but the self-invoice is still mandatorily required for GST purposes — the supplier's commercial invoice alone does not satisfy the RCM invoice obligation.

### Mandatory Fields in Self-Invoice

**Rule 46** specifies the mandatory contents of a tax invoice. For a self-invoice under Section 31(3)(f), the following fields are mandatory (as adapted for the RCM context where the recipient is the issuer):

| Sl. No. | Mandatory Field | Notes for Self-Invoice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Name, Address, GSTIN of the **recipient** (who is issuing the self-invoice) | The recipient's own details; their GSTIN goes here as the "supplier" in the RCM context |
| 2 | Sequential Serial Number | Must be a unique, consecutive serial number within the financial year (separate series recommended for self-invoices to avoid confusion with regular tax invoices) |
| 3 | Date of Issue | Date of the self-invoice — must be on or before the payment date or date of accounting, whichever is earlier |
| 4 | Name and Address of the **actual supplier** | For unregistered domestic supplier: name and address of the unregistered vendor. For foreign supplier: name and address of the foreign company |
| 5 | GSTIN of actual supplier | For unregistered domestic supplier: write "Unregistered" or leave blank. For foreign supplier: write "Overseas / Non-GST" |
| 6 | HSN Code of Goods / SAC Code of Services | 4/6/8 digit HSN for goods; SAC code for services |
| 7 | Description of Goods/Services | Full description of the supply received |
| 8 | Quantity (for goods) and Unit of Measurement | Required for goods; for services, description suffices |
| 9 | Value of Supply (taxable value) | The value of the supply (in INR; for foreign currency: converted at RBI reference rate) |
| 10 | Applicable GST Rate | CGST rate + SGST rate (for intra-state) or IGST rate (for inter-state/import) |
| 11 | Tax Amount | Computed GST (CGST + SGST or IGST) |
| 12 | Place of Supply | Important for determining CGST+SGST vs IGST |
| 13 | Whether the GST is payable on Reverse Charge | "Yes" — this confirms the RCM nature of the document |
| 14 | Signature of Authorised Signatory | Wet ink or digital signature of the authorised person of the recipient entity |

**Additional fields (where applicable):**
- For import of services: Name of country of foreign supplier; currency of original invoice; exchange rate applied.
- For transactions with GTA: Consignment note number; freight amount; goods description.
- For director's services: Director identification number (DIN); nature of services.

### Timing of Self-Invoice

**When must the self-invoice be issued?**

For RCM supplies (both Section 9(3) and 9(4) and import of services), the self-invoice must be issued:
- **On or before** the date of payment to the supplier, OR
- **On or before** the date of entry in the books of account (accounting date),
- whichever is earlier.

This ties the self-invoice issuance to the **time of supply** determination. The self-invoice date becomes the reference point for the GSTR-3B reporting period.

**Consequence of late self-invoice:** If a company makes a payment to an unregistered GTA on 5 March 2026 but issues the self-invoice on 15 April 2026, the self-invoice date (15 April) would place the RCM liability in April's return. However, technically the time of supply was 5 March (date of payment) and the GST was due in March's GSTR-3B. The late self-invoice does not shift the legal time of supply. Interest under Section 50 would run from the March due date.

**Best practice:** Issue self-invoices on the same day as payment (or as the accounting entry if booking accruals), to align the self-invoice date, accounting date, and payment date, thereby simplifying GSTR-3B reporting.

## Rule 52 — Payment Voucher
{law_reference: "Rule 52 CGST Rules, 2017; Section 31(3)(g) CGST Act, 2017"}

**Section 31(3)(g) of the CGST Act, 2017** requires:
> *"A registered person who is liable to pay tax under sub-section (3) or sub-section (4) of section 9 shall issue a payment voucher at the time of making payment to the supplier."*

**Rule 52** of the CGST Rules specifies the contents of a payment voucher.

**Purpose — distinction from self-invoice:**

| Aspect | Self-Invoice (Section 31(3)(f) + Rule 46) | Payment Voucher (Section 31(3)(g) + Rule 52) |
|---|---|---|
| Issued by | RCM recipient (registered person) | RCM recipient (registered person) |
| Issued when | On or before payment / accounting date | At the time of making payment to the supplier |
| Purpose | Serves as the tax invoice for RCM (document for ITC; document for GST reporting) | Documents the outward payment to the supplier; complements the self-invoice |
| ITC document | Yes — self-invoice is the ITC basis under Rule 36(3) | No — payment voucher is NOT the ITC document |
| Contains tax computation | Yes | Yes (mirrors self-invoice tax details) |

**Mandatory fields in a payment voucher (Rule 52):**

1. Name, address, GSTIN of the registered recipient (issuer of the voucher).
2. A consecutive serial number (separate from the self-invoice series; separate from the regular invoice series).
3. Date of issue of the payment voucher.
4. Name, address, and GSTIN (if any) of the supplier.
5. Description of goods or services.
6. Amount of taxable supply.
7. Tax rate and tax amount.
8. Place of supply.
9. Signature of authorised signatory.

**In practice:** Many businesses issue a **combined document** that serves as both the self-invoice and the payment voucher — a single document titled "Self-Invoice cum Payment Voucher" containing all required fields from both Rule 46 and Rule 52. While two separate documents are technically prescribed, the GST Council and department have accepted combined documents in practice, provided all mandatory fields are present.

**Timing importance:** The payment voucher must be issued "at the time of making payment." This is different from the self-invoice (which can be issued up to the date of accounting). In a scenario where goods/services are received and booked, but payment is made later:
- Self-invoice: issued at accounting date (e.g., 31 March when the provision is made).
- Payment voucher: issued on the payment date (e.g., 10 April when the bank transfer is made).

**Consequence of non-issuance:** Failure to issue a payment voucher is a violation of Section 31(3)(g) and attracts a penalty under Section 125 CGST Act (₹25,000 general penalty) or Section 122 (penalty for non-issue of invoice/voucher — up to 100% of tax or ₹10,000, whichever is higher).

## Supplementary: Section 31(3)(f) — Self-Invoice Obligation
{law_reference: "Section 31(3)(f) CGST Act, 2017; Section 31(3)(g) CGST Act, 2017; Rule 46 CGST Rules, 2017; Section 122 CGST Act, 2017; Section 125 CGST Act, 2017"}

### Full Statutory Text

**Section 31(3) of the CGST Act, 2017** reads (relevant clauses):

> *(3) A registered person shall issue, in the prescribed form and manner,—*
>
> *(f) where the supplier is not registered and the supplies are received by the registered person on which tax is payable on reverse charge, a self-invoice, for the value of the supply, within thirty days of the supply of goods or services or both;*
>
> *(g) a payment voucher at the time of making payment to the supplier.*

*[Note: The specific timeframe of "30 days" in the context of Section 31(3)(f) is subject to the Rule 46/Rule 47 framework; practitioners should verify the exact prescribed period under the applicable rules as of the relevant date.]*

### Obligation

**Who has the obligation?** The **registered person who is the RCM recipient** has the obligation to issue the self-invoice. There is no option to substitute another document (e.g., the supplier's commercial invoice or purchase order) for the self-invoice in the RCM context.

**What if the recipient fails to issue a self-invoice?**
- The ITC claim based on that supply is at risk — Rule 36(3) requires a self-invoice for ITC eligibility on RCM supplies.
- The RCM tax liability still exists and must be paid (with interest if late).
- Non-issuance of the self-invoice is itself a penalty provision breach.

**When the obligation arises:** On or before the time of supply (the earliest of the triggers discussed under Rule 5). Practically, the self-invoice should be issued on the date of payment or the date of accounting in the books, whichever comes first.

### Penalty for Non-Compliance

**Section 122 of the CGST Act, 2017 — specific penalty for invoice non-compliance:**

Section 122(1)(ii): A taxable person who issues an invoice or document by which an amount is made due without being a taxable person or without having a valid registration shall be liable to a penalty of ₹10,000 or the amount of tax evaded, whichever is higher.

Section 122(1)(i): A taxable person who supplies goods or services without issuing an invoice in violation of the provisions of this Act or the rules made thereunder shall be liable to a penalty of ₹10,000 or the amount of tax evaded, whichever is higher.

For the RCM context specifically — **non-issuance of self-invoice:**

1. **Penalty per Section 122:** ₹10,000 per instance of non-compliance (per self-invoice not issued) OR the amount of tax involved in that transaction, whichever is higher.

2. **Penalty per Section 125 (general penalty):** Where no specific penalty is prescribed, the general penalty is up to ₹25,000.

3. **Interest under Section 50:** On the RCM tax not paid (or paid late), at 18% per annum.

4. **Penalty for tax non-payment under Section 73/74:** If RCM is not paid and the department raises a demand:
   - Section 73 (without suppression/fraud): 10% of tax or ₹10,000, whichever is higher.
   - Section 74 (with suppression/fraud/wilful misstatement): up to 100% of tax.

**Cumulative penalty in a non-compliance scenario:**

> ABC Ltd. fails to issue self-invoices and pay RCM on director's fees for 2 years (FY 2023-24 and FY 2024-25). Total RCM liability: ₹2,16,000 (24 months × ₹9,000 per month).
>
> Department assessment:
> - Principal RCM tax: ₹2,16,000
> - Interest @ 18% p.a. for average 1 year (simplified): ₹38,880
> - Penalty u/s 122 (assuming 24 instances of self-invoice non-issuance): ₹10,000 × 24 = ₹2,40,000 (exceeds tax amount per instance in many months, so higher of ₹10,000 or tax applies)
> - Penalty u/s 73 (10% of tax, assuming no suppression): ₹21,600
> - Total exposure: ₹2,16,000 + ₹38,880 + ₹21,600 = ₹2,76,480 (exclusive of self-invoice non-issuance penalty which is separately computed)

**[MAHARASHTRA-SPECIFIC]** The Maharashtra GST department's **audit wing** has been particularly active in FY 2024-25 and 2025-26 in identifying RCM non-compliance, especially for:
1. Director's sitting fees RCM (highly prevalent non-compliance).
2. Security services from non-body-corporate agencies.
3. Residential property rentals by registered companies.

Maharashtra companies that are selected for GST audit (Section 65) or special audit (Section 66) should proactively review their self-invoice issuance records and RCM payment history for the audit period.

### How Self-Invoice Differs from Regular Tax Invoice

| Parameter | Regular Tax Invoice (Section 31(1)/(2)) | Self-Invoice (Section 31(3)(f)) |
|---|---|---|
| Issued by | **Supplier** of goods/services | **Recipient** of goods/services |
| GST registration of issuer | Registered supplier | Registered recipient |
| Reason for issuance | Normal taxable forward charge supply | RCM supply from unregistered / foreign supplier |
| Supplier details on document | Issuer's own details (as supplier) | Recipient's own details as issuer; actual supplier's details as counterparty |
| Tax charged to whom | Tax charged to recipient (buyer) | No tax charged to actual supplier; recipient self-assesses and pays RCM |
| Document for ITC (for recipient) | The supplier's tax invoice (Rule 36(1)(a)) | The recipient's own self-invoice (Rule 36(3)) |
| Numbering series | Regular invoice series | Separate series recommended (e.g., "SI-2025-26-001") |
| Filing in GST returns | Supplier reports in GSTR-1 outward; recipient sees in GSTR-2B | Recipient reports in GSTR-3B Table 3.1(d); self-invoice is not filed separately |
| Applicability of E-invoicing | Yes, for eligible turnover thresholds | Separate e-invoicing applicability for self-invoices (check current threshold; from 1 Aug 2023, e-invoicing threshold ₹5 crore turnover; self-invoice e-invoicing requirements have been extended to RCM recipients above threshold) |

**E-invoicing and self-invoices:**
As of **1 August 2023**, the e-invoicing requirement applies to registered persons with aggregate turnover exceeding ₹5 crore in any preceding financial year. For such entities, **self-invoices** (issued for RCM under Section 31(3)(f)) must also be generated as e-invoices on the IRP (Invoice Registration Portal) and carry an IRN (Invoice Reference Number) and QR code. This is a significant compliance obligation for large companies.

**[MAHARASHTRA-SPECIFIC]** Maharashtra's large corporate sector means most companies in Mumbai, Pune, Thane, Nagpur, and other major cities already exceed the ₹5 crore threshold and are subject to e-invoicing for their self-invoices. Failure to generate e-invoices for self-invoices (RCM) is treated as non-issuance of a valid invoice, with associated ITC disallowance risk.
