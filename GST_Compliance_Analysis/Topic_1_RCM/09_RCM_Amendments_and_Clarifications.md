## Introduction: The Role of CBIC Circulars in RCM Interpretation

The GST legislative framework — comprising the CGST Act 2017, IGST Act 2017, state SGST Acts, CGST Rules 2017, and the notifications issued under these enactments — is drafted at a level of generality that frequently leaves taxpayers and their advisors without clear guidance on specific fact patterns. Notifications issued under Section 9(3) (e.g., Notification No. 13/2017-CT(Rate)) list categories of RCM services using broad descriptions — "services of a goods transport agency," "legal services by an advocate" — without defining every term or resolving every ambiguity that arises in commercial reality. CBIC circulars and clarifications step into this gap, providing the department's interpretation of how the law should be applied to specific situations.

**Legal Status of CBIC Circulars:**

The binding force of CBIC circulars has been the subject of extensive litigation and Supreme Court pronouncements. The settled legal position, emerging from the Supreme Court's decisions in *Paper Products Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central Excise* (1999) 7 SCC 84 and *CCE v. Dhiren Chemical Industries* (2002) 2 SCC 30, is:

- Circulars issued by the CBIC are binding on the Department and all subordinate officers — a GST officer cannot take a position contrary to a valid CBIC circular. If a circular is beneficial to the taxpayer, the taxpayer can enforce it against the department.
- Circulars are not binding on the taxpayer or on courts. A taxpayer may adopt a position that is contrary to a circular, provided the position is supported by a correct reading of the statute, and defend it before adjudicating authorities, appellate tribunals, and courts.
- A circular that is contrary to the statute does not become law merely by virtue of having been issued by CBIC. Courts will strike it down if it exceeds the statutory framework.

**Practical Compliance Expectation:**

In practice, CBIC circulars set the compliance expectation for the vast majority of taxpayers who cannot afford or are not disposed to litigate against the department's position. Audit teams, adjudicating officers, and appellate authorities operate within the framework of CBIC circulars. Departing from a circular position — even for legally sound reasons — will typically result in demand issuance at the audit/assessment level, requiring the taxpayer to contest the matter before higher forums. For matters where the circular amount is below ₹5 lakh annually, the cost of litigation typically exceeds the tax amount, making circular compliance commercially rational even where the legal position may be debatable.

**Supreme Court's Final Authority:** Where a CBIC circular conflicts with the statute, the Supreme Court (and High Courts on writ) will apply the statute and not the circular. Notable RCM-related cases where the Supreme Court's statutory reading has prevailed over departmental/circular positions are referenced at relevant points in this document.

## Circular 164/20/2021-GST — Renting of Motor Vehicles
{amendment_date: "06-Oct-2021"}
{law_reference: "Circular No. 164/20/2021-GST dated 06-Oct-2021; Notification No. 29/2018-CT(Rate) dated 31-Dec-2018; Notification No. 12/2017-CT(Rate)"}

### Background and Legislative Context

Notification No. 29/2018-CT(Rate) added "renting of any motor vehicle designed to carry passengers where the cost of fuel is included in the consideration charged from the service recipient" to the list of RCM services under Notification No. 13/2017-CT(Rate). The entry specifically stated that RCM would apply when the supplier is "any person other than a body corporate" and the recipient is a "body corporate located in the taxable territory." This entry, inserted with effect from 1 January 2019, generated immediate and extensive confusion about its precise scope.

### The Central Question

The notification appeared to impose RCM only when the supplier is a non-body-corporate (individual, partnership, LLP) renting a vehicle to a body corporate recipient. But two significant ambiguities arose:

1. If both supplier and recipient are body corporates — what rate and what charge mechanism applies?
2. What about cab aggregators (Ola, Uber) who use a digital platform to connect riders with individual drivers? Is the aggregator's service "renting of motor vehicle"?

Prior to Circular 164/20/2021, different Advance Ruling Authorities had taken inconsistent positions:
- *Kerala AAR (M/s Baby Marine Exports):* Held that renting of motor vehicle from an individual operator to a body corporate attracts 12% GST on forward charge (not RCM at 5%)
- *Karnataka AAR:* Held differently on the forward charge / RCM split for the same type of transaction
- *Multiple Maharashtra AAR rulings:* Applied the notification text literally but differed on what constitutes "cost of fuel included in consideration"

### CBIC Clarification — Circular 164/20/2021-GST

**Clarification 1: Body Corporate to Body Corporate — 12% Forward Charge**

When a body corporate (company, LLP is specifically excluded as explained below) provides motor vehicle renting services to another body corporate, and both are registered under GST, the applicable tax is 12% on forward charge. The supplier (body corporate) is liable to pay the tax. RCM does not apply. The entry in Notification 13/2017-CT(Rate) covers only non-body-corporate suppliers; when the supplier is itself a body corporate, the RCM entry is inapplicable.

This has significant practical implications:
- Corporate fleet operators, car rental companies (body corporates) providing vehicles to corporate clients → 12% GST; corporate client claims ITC at 12%
- The client cannot treat this as RCM and avoid cash payment

**Clarification 2: Individual Operator to Body Corporate — 5% RCM**

When the supplier is an individual person (or a non-corporate entity such as a partnership firm, a HUF, a proprietorship) renting a motor vehicle to a body corporate recipient, RCM applies at 5% (without ITC for the supplier). The body corporate recipient pays 5% IGST/CGST+SGST under RCM.

**Clarification 3: Cab Aggregators**

The circular addressed the position of cab aggregators (Ola, Uber, and similar platforms):
- Aggregator's platform charges (commission, subscription fees, booking fees): these are services of an "electronic commerce operator" / intermediary, taxable at 18% on forward charge under the aggregator's GST registration
- The individual cab driver's service of transporting passengers: the aggregator may be liable to pay GST on behalf of the driver under Section 9(5) CGST Act (operator liable for specified services supplied through electronic commerce operators)
- However, if a corporate entity is renting vehicles from individual drivers through an aggregator platform for employee transportation (as opposed to individual passenger booking): separate analysis required based on contract structure

**Industry Impact:**

The circular resolved the forward-charge vs RCM confusion for the large segment of corporate India that uses organised fleet management companies (body corporates) for employee transportation. Pre-circular, many such companies were erroneously paying 5% RCM instead of claiming ITC on 12% forward charge from the fleet company — resulting in higher effective cost (no ITC on 5% RCM for certain blocked categories, vs full ITC on 12% forward charge).

### Remaining Ambiguity Post-Circular

**LLP Suppliers:** The Companies Act definition of "body corporate" (Section 2(11)) includes companies and certain other corporate bodies but explicitly excludes Limited Liability Partnerships. However, LLPs are registered corporate entities under the LLP Act, 2008. Whether LLPs constitute "body corporates" for the purpose of Notification 29/2018-CT(Rate) remains debated:
- Some AARs (including certain Karnataka AARs post-circular) hold LLPs are NOT body corporates → LLP renting motor vehicle to body corporate → 5% RCM applies
- The Companies Act definition (which the CGST Act may adopt by reference) supports this position
- However, the IGST Act's Section 2 cross-references suggest broader interpretation

**Tax Planning Risk:** Businesses that structure their fleet operations through LLPs to maintain the 5% RCM option (rather than 12% forward charge) should note that if the department takes the position that the LLP IS a body corporate, 12% forward charge would apply — and if 5% RCM was paid instead, the shortfall (7% gross-up tax) would be demanded from the LLP supplier. This is an under-appreciated reverse risk.

**HUF and Trust Operators:** Individual driver partnerships operating as HUFs or trusts similarly fall outside the "body corporate" definition → 5% RCM applies when renting to a body corporate.

## Circular 177/09/2022-GST — Directors, Advocates, and Related Matters
{amendment_date: "09-Sep-2022"}
{law_reference: "Circular No. 177/09/2022-GST dated 09-Sep-2022; Notification No. 13/2017-CT(Rate) dated 28-Jun-2017; Schedule III CGST Act, 2017"}

### Background

Two categories of RCM — director services and advocate services — generated disproportionate compliance confusion relative to their transactional volume. Every company with non-executive directors, every business using legal counsel, and every business receiving services from management consultants faced daily uncertainty on RCM applicability. Circular 177/09/2022-GST was issued to provide definitive clarification on several questions that had accumulated over five years of GST implementation.

### Clarification 1: Director Services — Employment vs Professional Engagement

**The Statutory Framework:**

Notification No. 13/2017-CT(Rate) Entry 6 imposes RCM on "services supplied by a director of a company or a body corporate to the said company or the body corporate." This applies when the director supplies services in their professional capacity.

Schedule III of the CGST Act lists activities that are neither a supply of goods nor a supply of services — and are therefore entirely outside GST. Entry 1 of Schedule III covers "services by an employee to the employer in the course of or in relation to his employment." If a director's services constitute employment services, they are Schedule III excluded and attract no GST whatsoever.

**CBIC's Clarification in Circular 177:**

The circular articulates a definitive test for distinguishing employment from professional service:

*Test of Employer-Employee Relationship:* Where a director has an employment contract with the company, is subject to the company's supervisory authority, receives fixed monthly salary, is enrolled in the company's PF and ESI schemes (where applicable), and the relationship has the essential characteristics of a service relationship in labour law terms → the engagement is employment → Schedule III exclusion → NO GST.

*Test of Professional Engagement:* Where a director is engaged on a retainer basis, charges a professional fee (commission, sitting fees, advisory charges), issues invoices or relies on the company's resolution for payment, is not subject to the PF/ESI regime, and the engagement can be characterised as that of an independent contractor → the engagement is a professional service → RCM at 18% IGST or CGST+SGST → company pays.

**Split Treatment for Directors with Dual Compensation:**

Many companies, particularly listed public companies, pay their directors:
- Salary under an employment contract (whole-time director / executive director / managing director)
- Sitting fees / commission as authorised under the Companies Act, 2013 and the Articles of Association

The circular confirms that split treatment is appropriate:
- Salary component → employment → Schedule III → no GST
- Sitting fees and commission component → professional service → 18% RCM on company

**Documentation Required:**

Following Circular 177, the appropriate documentation package for director remuneration includes:
- Board resolution or appointment letter establishing whether the director is executive (employed) or non-executive (professional)
- Copy of employment contract (Form 16 PF/ESI registration as corroborating documents) for whole-time directors
- Company's Articles of Association provisions for commission/sitting fees
- Separate payment vouchers/self-invoices for the professional fee component
- Self-invoice for each sitting fee / commission payment (since the company is the RCM payer)

**Maharashtra SGST Alignment:**

**[MAHARASHTRA-SPECIFIC]** Prior to Circular 177, Maharashtra SGST officers in certain assessment proceedings had taken the position that all director remuneration — including salary paid to executive directors — attracted RCM at 18%, unless the company could produce specific evidence of an employment relationship. This position (which conflated professional engagement and employment) was effectively overruled by Circular 177. Post-circular, Maharashtra SGST officers are required to follow the employment test articulated in the circular. However, pending assessments and appeals for pre-circular periods (FY 2017-18 to FY 2021-22) in Maharashtra continue to be contested based on the earlier, more aggressive departmental position.

### Clarification 2: Advocate Services

**Coverage of "Firm of Advocates":**

Notification No. 13/2017-CT(Rate) Entry 2 covers "services supplied by an individual advocate including a senior advocate by way of legal services, directly or indirectly, to any business entity." A long-standing question was whether a firm of advocates (a partnership of individual advocates) was covered. Circular 177 clarified:

*Firm of Advocates:* RCM applies. The firm is a grouping of individual advocates; when the firm provides legal services to a business entity, the underlying service is supplied by individual advocates acting through their partnership. RCM applies on the business entity recipient.

*Senior Advocate Directly Engaging:* Confirmed to be covered under the individual advocate entry → RCM applies.

*Advocate Providing Services to Non-Business Entities (individuals):* Not covered by the notification (notification specifies "business entity" as recipient) → no RCM; no GST if aggregate turnover below threshold; forward charge if threshold crossed.

**Legal Consultants vs Advocates:**

A "legal consultant" who is not a qualified advocate enrolled with a State Bar Council is NOT covered by Entry 2 of Notification 13/2017-CT(Rate). Such consultants (retired bureaucrats with legal expertise, non-advocate legal researchers, compliance specialists) provide services that, if taxable, are on forward charge. The RCM entry specifically covers "advocates" — a term defined in the Advocates Act, 1961.

**Foreign Law Firms:**

Foreign law firms providing legal opinions, contract reviews, or jurisdiction-specific advice to Indian businesses are not enrolled as advocates in India and therefore not covered by Entry 2 of Notification 13/2017-CT(Rate). However, their services constitute an "import of service" → Section 5(3) IGST Act read with Notification 10/2017-IT(Rate) applies, triggering IGST RCM on the Indian recipient under the general import-of-service framework, not under the advocate-specific entry. The rate is 18% IGST.

## Notification No. 13/2017-CT(Rate) — Master Notification for Section 9(3) Services
{amendment_date: "28-Jun-2017 (as amended through May 2026)"}
{law_reference: "Notification No. 13/2017-CT(Rate) dated 28-Jun-2017 (G.S.R. 694(E)); IGST equivalent: Notification No. 10/2017-IT(Rate) dated 28-Jun-2017"}

### The Master Notification

Notification No. 13/2017-CT(Rate) is the primary legislative instrument listing the categories of services subject to RCM under Section 9(3) of the CGST Act. It has been amended multiple times since its original issuance. The IGST equivalent (Notification No. 10/2017-IT(Rate)) mirrors the same categories for inter-state supplies. State notifications (issued by each state government under the respective SGST Act) mirror the CGST notification for intra-state supplies.

**Original Notification (28-Jun-2017) — Core Entries:**

| Entry | Service Category | Recipient Liable for RCM |
|-------|-----------------|--------------------------|
| 1 | GTA services to specified persons | Specified recipients (factories, societies, body corporates, partnerships, casual taxable persons, persons in specified categories) |
| 2 | Legal services by individual advocate / senior advocate | Any business entity |
| 3 | Services by an arbitral tribunal | Any business entity |
| 4 | Services by a partnership firm of advocates | Any business entity |
| 5 | Services by Central / State Government, UT or local authority (excluding specified services) | Any business entity |
| 6 | Services by a director to a company or body corporate | The company or body corporate |
| 7 | Services by an insurer to an insured in re-insurance | Any insurer |
| 8 | Services by a recovery agent to a banking company or NBFC | The banking company or NBFC |
| 9 | Services by author, music composer, photographer, artist — copyright assignment — to publisher, music company, producer | Publisher, music company, producer |
| 10 | Services provided by the members of overseeing committee of RBI | RBI |

**Key Amendments to Notification 13/2017-CT(Rate):**

**2018 Amendments:**

*Notification No. 29/2018-CT(Rate) (31-Dec-2018):* Added Entry 15 — Renting of motor vehicle designed to carry passengers (seating capacity ≤ 13 persons, including driver), where the cost of fuel is included in the consideration, by any person other than a body corporate to a body corporate in the taxable territory, at 5% RCM without ITC for supplier. This was the amendment that precipitated Circular 164/20/2021 (discussed above).

*Security Services Entry (2018):* Added services provided by way of supply of security personnel by any person other than a body corporate to any registered person located in the taxable territory. This brought the unorganised security services sector into RCM compliance.

**2019 Amendments:**

*Restoration of Section 9(4) Categories under Notification 7/2019-CT(Rate):* After the suspension of the broad Section 9(4) in 2018, Notification 7/2019-CT(Rate) restored Section 9(4) RCM for specific goods and services categories, including services provided by unregistered persons in specific notified categories.

*GTA Modifications:* Refined the list of "specified persons" who trigger RCM for GTA services. Added clarity on what constitutes a consignment note (the trigger document for GTA classification).

**2022 Amendments — GTA Forward Charge Option:**

*Notification No. 03/2022-CT(Rate) (effective 18-Jul-2022):* This is the most significant amendment to the GTA entry since 2017. GTAs can now opt to pay GST at 12% (with full ITC benefit) under forward charge, instead of the recipient paying 5% RCM. The option must be exercised by filing a declaration with the jurisdictional tax authority by 15 March of the year preceding the financial year from which the option is to be exercised. Detailed provisions discussed under the dedicated GTA section below.

**2024-25 Amendments:**

Any additions or modifications made through Finance Act 2024 or notifications issued during FY 2024-25 should be verified against the official Gazette notifications. The following general updates are noted:
- Adjustments to the copyright services entry to align with the Copyright Act amendments
- Clarification circulars on the scope of "business entity" for the advocate and arbitral tribunal entries
- Alignment of recovery agent services with updated RBI and SEBI frameworks

**2025-26 Amendments (up to May 2026):**

As of May 2026, practitioners should verify the latest notification text on the CBIC website (www.cbic.gov.in) or the GST Portal's notification repository. The Finance Act 2025/2026 and any mid-year notifications may have modified the notification. This document provides the framework as of May 2026; specific notification text must always be verified against the official Gazette.

## GTA Amendments — Forward Charge Option (Effective 2022)
{amendment_date: "18-Jul-2022 (effective for FY 2023-24 onwards with option exercise by 15-Mar-2023)"}
{law_reference: "Notification No. 03/2022-CT(Rate) dated 13-Jul-2022; Notification No. 11/2017-CT(Rate) as amended; Circular No. 164/20/2021-GST (context); Rule 7 CGST Rules (composition — not directly applicable but analogous); GST Portal functionality for GTA option declaration"}

### The Major 2022 Reform

Prior to this amendment, GTAs were locked into the 5% RCM structure (recipient pays) with no ITC eligibility for the GTA (if the 5% rate was chosen). GTAs that had significant ITC accumulation (purchase of trucks, fuel, tyres, workshop services) could not utilise this ITC because their output liability was effectively zero (recipients paid the tax). This created a perverse incentive where GTAs with large ITC balances had idle credits that were commercially worthless.

The 2022 amendment gave GTAs two options:
1. **Retain RCM:** Continue with the existing structure — recipient pays 5% GST, GTA has no GST liability, GTA cannot claim ITC
2. **Opt for Forward Charge at 12%:** GTA pays 12% GST (with full ITC benefit), recipient does NOT pay any RCM, recipient claims ITC on GTA's tax invoice as regular forward-charge ITC

### Procedure for GTA to Exercise the Forward Charge Option

**Step 1 — Annual Declaration Filing:** The GTA must file an annual declaration in the prescribed form with its jurisdictional GST officer. The declaration must be filed by 15 March of the year preceding the financial year for which the option is exercised.

- To be on forward charge for FY 2025-26: declaration must have been filed by 15 March 2025
- To be on forward charge for FY 2026-27: declaration must be filed by 15 March 2026

**Step 2 — Portal Reflection:** The GST portal is expected to reflect the GTA's option status in its profile. Buyers can verify the GTA's forward charge status on the portal.

**Step 3 — Annual Renewal:** The option must be renewed annually. A GTA that has opted for forward charge for FY 2025-26 is not automatically on forward charge for FY 2026-27 — a fresh declaration by 15 March 2026 is required.

### Implications for Recipients of GTA Services

**If GTA has opted for forward charge:**
- GTA issues a tax invoice showing 12% GST (5% CGST + 5% SGST for intra-state, or 12% IGST for inter-state)
- Recipient does NOT pay any RCM; no self-invoice required for this supply
- Recipient claims ITC on GTA's tax invoice in GSTR-3B Table 4A(1) or 4A(5) — as regular forward-charge ITC
- Recipient's GSTR-2B will auto-populate this credit from GTA's GSTR-1

**If GTA has NOT opted for forward charge (default position):**
- GTA issues a freight bill / consignment note without GST
- Recipient (if it is a "specified person" covered by Notification 13/2017) pays 5% RCM
- Recipient issues self-invoice, pays cash, claims ITC (subject to Rule 36(3) timing)

**Verification Obligation on Recipients:** Recipients cannot assume all GTAs have opted for forward charge. The default position is RCM. The burden is on the recipient to confirm the GTA's forward charge status before treating the supply as forward charge. Recommended approach:
- At the beginning of each financial year, send a verification request to all GTA vendors asking for confirmation of their forward charge status for the year
- Document the GTA's response (email / letter)
- If GTA fails to respond, assume RCM default and pay accordingly

### GTAs with ITC — Economic Analysis

GTAs that have invested in fleet (trucks, trailers) and claim ITC on repairs, tyres, fuel, and vehicle purchases benefit significantly from the forward charge option:
- Under RCM (old): GTA accumulates ITC with no output tax to set off → refund of accumulated ITC (possible but slow)
- Under forward charge (new): GTA applies accumulated ITC against its 12% output liability → net tax payable after ITC set-off; excess ITC eligible for refund as inverted duty structure

For large fleet operators, the forward charge option can significantly improve cash flow and reduce effective tax cost. However, for small GTAs with minimal ITC and high use of exempt route categories, the 5% RCM (with zero administrative burden on the GTA) may remain commercially preferable.

## Circular 196/8/2023-GST — Employee Secondment and Cross-Border Staff Arrangements
{amendment_date: "2023"}
{law_reference: "Circular No. 196/8/2023-GST; Section 5(3) IGST Act, 2017; Section 2(11) IGST Act (import of services definition); Schedule III CGST Act, 2017"}

### Background — The Secondment Dilemma

Employee secondment — where a foreign group company seconds one or more of its employees to work for the Indian subsidiary — is a common arrangement in multinational corporations (MNCs) across sectors (IT, banking, manufacturing, retail). The seconded employee works in India, is supervised by the Indian entity, serves the Indian entity's business needs, but technically remains on the payroll of the foreign group company.

The GST implications turned on a fundamental question: Does the foreign entity supply "services" to the Indian entity, or is the arrangement simply an employment relationship (which would be excluded from GST under Schedule III)?

**Pre-Circular Chaos:** Prior to Circular 196/8/2023, AAR rulings across different states were deeply inconsistent:
- Some AARs held that salary reimbursement = service supply = import of service = IGST RCM on Indian entity
- Others held that if the Indian entity "controls" the employee (direction and supervision), there is no supply of services by the foreign entity → no GST
- Some distinguished between "lien holder" arrangements (employment contract with foreign entity) and "deputation" (complete transfer of employment to Indian entity)
- Litigation was extensive; assessments ran into hundreds of crores across industries

### CBIC Clarification in Circular 196/8/2023

**Position 1 — Cost Recovery Creates Supply:**

If the foreign entity recovers from the Indian entity the cost of the seconded employee (salary, allowances, benefits, employer PF contributions, insurance, and any management charge), this recovery is consideration for a supply of manpower / employee services. The foreign entity is "supplying" the services of the employee to the Indian entity. Consequently:
- It is an import of service by the Indian entity
- IGST RCM is payable on the Indian entity
- Taxable value = amount recovered by the foreign entity from the Indian entity
- If the foreign entity issues an invoice, that invoice value is the taxable value; if no formal invoice, the actual cost recovery amount serves as the basis

**Position 2 — No Recovery, No Supply:**

If the foreign entity seconds an employee to the Indian entity but makes no recovery from the Indian entity (the entire cost is borne by the foreign entity), there is no consideration flowing from the Indian entity. Without consideration, there is no "supply" (subject to the deemed supply provisions). In this scenario, no IGST RCM is applicable on the Indian entity.

The crucial caveat: "no recovery" must be genuinely no recovery — not a nominal or token recovery. The department will examine whether the foreign entity has any mechanism to charge back the employee costs through transfer pricing adjustments, management fees, or service fee arrangements that functionally achieve a cost recovery without explicitly calling it secondment cost recovery.

**Position 3 — The Transfer Pricing Complication:**

Many MNCs compute intercompany charges through transfer pricing (TP) benchmarking that may allocate employee costs across group entities based on time, services rendered, or benefit test. If such TP allocations are made and include employee costs, the department's position under Circular 196 is that these allocations constitute recoveries → import of service → IGST RCM.

This position creates an overlap between TP documentation (for income tax) and GST documentation: TP study reports may inadvertently document cost recoveries that the Indian entity had not treated as imports of service for GST purposes.

### Industry Significance

Circular 196/8/2023-GST has had profound impact on:
- **IT Sector:** Large IT companies in Maharashtra (TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCL) regularly second employees both ways (inbound and outbound). Inbound secondments with cost recovery from the Indian company → IGST RCM on Indian entity
- **Banking and Financial Services:** Foreign banks seconds senior management to Indian branches; recovery from Indian entity → import of service under Circular 196
- **Manufacturing MNCs:** Technical experts seconded from German or Japanese parent companies; technical assistance agreements often bundle employee costs with equipment licensing → complex apportionment

**Maharashtra Exposure:** **[MAHARASHTRA-SPECIFIC]** Maharashtra houses a very high concentration of MNC subsidiaries and IT sector companies. Maharashtra SGST audit teams have specifically used Circular 196 as a basis to raise demands in high-value taxpayer audits (taxpayers above ₹100 crore turnover). Assessments for pre-circular periods (FY 2017-18 to FY 2022-23) continue to be adjudicated based on the principles now articulated in the circular, applied retrospectively to the extent permissible under the statute of limitations.

## Circular 211/5/2024-GST — RCM Clarifications on Specific Categories
{amendment_date: "2024"}
{law_reference: "Circular No. 211/5/2024-GST (and other 2024 CBIC circulars); relevant notifications"}

### Context and Scope

CBIC Circular 211/5/2024-GST (and related 2024 circulars issued in the same period) addressed several accumulated questions on RCM applicability and procedural issues that had arisen following the GTA forward charge reforms of 2022 and the Circular 196 secondment clarification of 2023. Practitioners should verify the exact text of this circular from the CBIC website, as the summary below captures the principal clarifications:

**Clarification on Security Services — Threshold for RCM:**

Security services from unregistered persons under Section 9(4) / Notification 7/2019-CT(Rate) continued to generate questions about what constitutes a "registered person" supplier (who would be covered by Section 9(3) / Notification 13/2017) vs an "unregistered person" supplier (Section 9(4)). The 2024 circular:
- Confirmed that where a security agency is registered under GST, the services are under Section 9(3) (if the agency is covered by the forward charge mechanism) or are a regular taxable supply on forward charge
- Reiterated that services from genuinely unregistered security providers remain under Section 9(4) RCM

**Clarification on E-Commerce Operators — Section 9(5) vs Section 9(3) RCM:**

Section 9(5) of the CGST Act makes the e-commerce operator liable to pay GST for certain services supplied through the platform (transportation of passengers, housekeeping, restaurant services). The 2024 circular clarified the interaction between:
- Section 9(5) (e-commerce operator liable) and
- Section 9(3) / 9(4) RCM (recipient liable)

where the same transaction could theoretically fall under both provisions. The general principle: Section 9(5) is a special provision that overrides the general Section 9(3)/9(4) where the e-commerce platform is the liable party.

**Author / Artist / Copyright Entry — Clarification:**

The 2024 circular clarified aspects of Entry 9 in Notification 13/2017-CT(Rate) (copyright assignment by authors, composers, etc.):
- The entry applies only to the first assignment of copyright from the creator to the publisher/music company/producer
- Sub-licensing or further assignment by the publisher to third parties is NOT covered by the RCM entry — it is on forward charge
- This distinction had been litigated in several cases involving music royalties and streaming platforms

**Procedural Clarifications:**

- Self-invoice date tolerance: where a self-invoice is issued within 30 days of the supply (not strictly on the date of supply), no penalty will be imposed provided the RCM tax was paid in the correct period based on the supply date. This administrative relaxation was welcomed by smaller businesses that process vendor payments on monthly cycles.
- Payment voucher for advances: reiterated that payment vouchers must be issued at the time of advance payment, not when the supply is eventually completed.

### Amendments to Section 9(3) and Section 9(4) CGST Act

**Section 9(3) Modifications:**

Finance Act 2025 may have expanded the categories of services notifiable under Section 9(3) to include emerging service categories relevant to the digital economy. As of May 2026, practitioners should check the Finance Act 2025 text for:

- Any revisions to the RCM payment mechanism (e.g., portal-level changes to prevent ITC from being offset against RCM liability)
- Whether Section 9(4) has been further expanded for specific unregistered supplier categories

**GST Council Decisions (44th and 45th Council Meetings):**

The GST Council meets approximately every three months. Decisions from the GST Council meetings in early 2026 (if any) that affect RCM should be tracked through:
- Press releases issued after each GST Council meeting
- Follow-up notifications from CBIC implementing the Council's decisions
- CBIC Circular explanations of new decisions

## ICAI Guidance on RCM for Professional Services

The Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI) has issued guidance on GST compliance for its members and for client advisory purposes. The following ICAI publications are directly relevant to RCM:

**ICAI Technical Guide on GST for Service Sector (Latest Edition):**

The Technical Guide covers RCM for service sector entities, including:
- Identification of RCM trigger points in service businesses (legal fees, consulting, security, transport)
- Guidance on self-invoice format and documentation
- Reconciliation methodology for GSTR-3B and GSTR-9

**ICAI's Guidance on RCM Identification for CA Firms:**

CA firms themselves receive several RCM-covered services:
- Legal counsel (advocate fees) → 18% RCM on the CA firm
- Security guards at office → from unregistered provider → RCM
- GTA services if the firm uses road transport for client delivery of documents or physical goods

ICAI has advised member firms to maintain a "RCM Threshold Calendar" — a monthly review of all vendor payments to identify RCM obligations, which is a best practice for any professional services firm.

**ICAI Recommended Internal Controls for RCM Compliance:**

1. **RCM Matrix:** Prepare a vendor categorisation matrix at the beginning of each financial year classifying all regular vendors as: (a) forward charge, (b) RCM — registered supplier, (c) RCM — unregistered supplier, (d) RCM — import of service
2. **Automated Self-Invoice Generation:** Integrate self-invoice generation with the accounts payable workflow — trigger on vendor payment approval
3. **Monthly RCM Checklist:** Pre-return filing checklist verifying: all RCM transactions identified; self-invoices generated; cash ledger balance sufficient; challan paid; ITC claimed only after payment confirmed
4. **Annual RCM Review:** Before GSTR-9 filing, reconcile cumulative RCM liability and ITC across 12 months; identify timing gaps; prepare explanation for GSTR-9C
5. **Vendor Registration Monitoring:** Quarterly check on unregistered vendor GST registration status (vendors crossing the threshold must be transitioned from RCM to forward charge)

## **[MAHARASHTRA-SPECIFIC]** Maharashtra SGST Notifications and Circulars
{law_reference: "Maharashtra SGST Act, 2017 (MAHA Act No. XLIII of 2017); Maharashtra GST Rules, 2017; Notifications issued under MGST Act by the Maharashtra Government; MGST Circulars issued by the Commissioner of State Tax, Maharashtra"}

### Maharashtra Government Notifications Mirroring CGST Notifications

Under the GST framework, the SGST component requires state governments to issue parallel notifications mirroring the CBIC/CGST notifications. Maharashtra has consistently issued parallel notifications under the MGST Act for all CGST notifications including:
- Maharashtra counterpart of Notification No. 13/2017-CT(Rate) for RCM services
- Maharashtra counterpart of Notification No. 29/2018-CT(Rate) for motor vehicle renting
- Maharashtra counterpart of Notification No. 03/2022-CT(Rate) for GTA forward charge

**[MAHARASHTRA-SPECIFIC]** Practitioners should verify the Maharashtra Government Gazette (available on mahaonline.gov.in or the Maharashtra GST Department's official website at gst.maharashtra.gov.in) for the specific dates on which Maharashtra notifications were issued. In several instances, Maharashtra notifications were issued a few days after the corresponding CBIC notifications, creating a brief gap period where the CGST RCM applied but the SGST RCM obligation was technically not yet notified. The department has treated these gap periods as administrative, not substantive, differences.

### Maharashtra Commissioner Circulars on RCM

The Commissioner of State Tax, Maharashtra has issued several Trade Circulars (TC) that provide state-level guidance:

**Trade Circular 19T of 2019 — Unregistered Person Supplies:**

Following the restoration of Section 9(4) through Notification 7/2019-CT(Rate), the Maharashtra Commissioner issued Trade Circular 19T of 2019 providing state-level guidance on how the restored Section 9(4) applies to purchases from unregistered persons in Maharashtra. The circular reiterated the CBIC position but included examples specific to Maharashtra trade patterns (agricultural produce traders, small construction contractors).

**Maharashtra-Specific Construction RCM Guidance:**

**[MAHARASHTRA-SPECIFIC]** Maharashtra's real estate sector prompted the state tax authority to issue specific guidance on construction-related RCM:
- Construction services from unregistered sub-contractors to registered developers: RCM applies
- Labour-only contracts vs composite contracts for construction: distinction for RCM rate determination
- Works contracts for immovable property: ITC blocked under Section 17(5)(c) and (d) — the state circular reinforced this block and alerted taxpayers to the common error of claiming RCM ITC on immovable property construction

### Dual Assessment Risk — CBIC Circular vs MGST Circular Conflict

**When CBIC Circular and State Circular Conflict:**

In the GST framework, the law is uniform (same CGST/IGST law nationwide), and CBIC circulars explain the law. State circulars should interpret the SGST provisions (which mirror the CGST provisions). In theory, there should be no conflict — both the CGST and SGST provisions are identically worded. However, conflicts can arise when:
- The state's Trade Circular interprets a term differently from CBIC
- A state AAR rules contrary to CBIC circular and the state tax authority applies the AAR
- Maharashtra SGST officers assess based on a pre-circular departmental position that has not been formally withdrawn

**Which Prevails?**

The law is clear: an AAR ruling is binding only on the applicant and the jurisdictional officer for that applicant. It is not binding on other taxpayers or on the state at large. A CBIC circular (binding on the department) takes precedence over a state-level Trade Circular or an AAR ruling when both concern the identical provision of CGST law. For SGST-specific provisions (which are identically worded), the state Commissioner's circular governs state assessment, but if the identical CGST provision has been clarified by CBIC, state officers are expected to follow the same interpretation.

**Practical Risk for Maharashtra Taxpayers:**

**[MAHARASHTRA-SPECIFIC]** A Maharashtra taxpayer assessed by the Maharashtra SGST authority based on a Maharashtra AAR ruling (which differs from CBIC circular) faces a peculiar position:
- The central officer (CGST Commissionerate) would apply the CBIC circular → no demand / different demand
- The state officer (MGST) would apply the Maharashtra AAR → demand raised

This creates dual (but differently-computed) assessments for the same transaction. The resolution typically requires filing a writ petition before the Bombay High Court seeking uniform application of the CBIC circular. The Bombay High Court has generally been sympathetic to the taxpayer's position that CBIC circulars, being binding on all GST officers (both central and state), should prevail over AAR rulings in matters of general legal interpretation.
