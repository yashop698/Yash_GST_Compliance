# Reverse Charge Mechanism (RCM) — Overview
{complexity_level: "Advanced"}
{topic: "RCM"}
{jurisdiction: "CGST Act 2017 + IGST Act 2017 + Maharashtra SGST"}

## Executive Summary
{law_reference: "Section 9(3), 9(4) CGST Act, 2017; Section 5(3), 5(4) IGST Act, 2017"}

The Reverse Charge Mechanism (RCM) is a statutory departure from the ordinary forward-charge principle of GST, under which the liability to pay tax to the government shifts from the supplier to the recipient of goods or services. Ordinarily, the supplier of a taxable supply collects GST from the buyer, deposits it with the government, and the buyer claims Input Tax Credit (ITC). Under RCM, this chain is inverted: the supplier neither collects nor pays the tax; instead the registered recipient self-assesses the liability, discharges it exclusively from the cash ledger (not from available ITC balance), and thereafter becomes entitled to claim ITC on the amount so paid — subject to fulfillment of all conditions under Sections 16, 17, and the relevant rules. RCM is triggered in two distinct situations: (a) when a supply falls within a category notified by the Central Government under Section 9(3)/5(3), regardless of the registration status of the supplier; and (b) when a registered person receives supplies of goods or services from an unregistered person and such categories are covered under Section 9(4)/5(4) notifications. Failure to self-assess and discharge RCM liability renders the recipient liable for tax, interest under Section 50 at 18% per annum, and penalties under Sections 73/74. The ITC consequent on RCM payment is available only in the tax period in which the payment is actually made to the government, creating a timing mismatch between liability recognition and credit availability.

## Legislative Intent — Why RCM Exists
{law_reference: "Section 9(3), 9(4) CGST Act, 2017"}

The introduction of Reverse Charge Mechanism into the GST framework was driven by four interlocking legislative purposes, each addressing a distinct structural vulnerability in the tax system:

**1. Plugging Compliance Gaps — Unreachable Suppliers**
Certain classes of suppliers are structurally incapable of being brought within the ordinary GST compliance net. Unregistered persons (with aggregate turnover below the threshold or voluntarily outside the system), foreign service providers with no establishment in India, and individual professionals who have historically operated in the informal economy cannot be effectively compelled to register, collect, and remit GST. Without RCM, any transaction with such suppliers would entirely escape the GST net at the supply stage. By shifting the obligation to the recipient — who is registered, visible in the system, and already subject to GST audit — the government ensures that the tax is collected even when the supplier is beyond reach.

**2. Revenue Assurance — Continuity of Collection**
The government's revenue interest is protected irrespective of the supplier's compliance behaviour. In a forward-charge world, if a supplier collects GST but fails to deposit it ("missing trader" fraud, a serious problem in pre-GST VAT regimes), the government loses revenue while having to honor the recipient's ITC claim. RCM eliminates this risk for the covered categories: the registered recipient pays directly into the government's account through the cash ledger, and ITC flows only after actual payment. There is no intermediary who can abscond with collected tax.

**3. Anti-Evasion Deterrence — Closing the Unregistered-Supplier Arbitrage**
Prior to Section 9(4) and its predecessors under the Service Tax regime (Section 68(2) of the Finance Act, 1994), businesses had a commercial incentive to prefer unregistered suppliers precisely because their supplies did not attract tax. This created a systemic distortion favouring informality. RCM removes this incentive: purchasing from an unregistered supplier (in notified categories) does not reduce the recipient's tax outflow; it merely transfers the obligation. For recipients engaged in making taxable supplies, the ITC offset means economic neutrality, but the cash-flow friction of advance payment from the cash ledger remains a deterrent against structuring transactions to exploit the unregistered-supplier route.

**4. Administrative Efficiency — Burdening the Regulated Party**
Registered recipients are already within the GST compliance ecosystem. They file returns, maintain books of accounts, are subject to audit, and have a GST officer assigned. Placing the RCM compliance obligation on such recipients — rather than attempting to register and monitor millions of small suppliers, foreign entities, or individual service providers — is administratively rational. The compliance cost is borne by parties who already bear it, and the government achieves comprehensive tax coverage without proportionate expansion of its enforcement machinery.

## Constitutional Basis
{law_reference: "Article 246A, Constitution of India; Entry 92C, List I, Seventh Schedule; GST (101st Constitutional Amendment) Act, 2016"}

The constitutional authority for the GST — and by extension for RCM — is Article 246A, inserted by the Constitution (101st Amendment) Act, 2016. Article 246A grants Parliament and State Legislatures concurrent power to make laws with respect to goods and services tax. The subject matter of the levy is "supply" of goods or services or both. RCM does not create a new or different taxable event; the taxable event remains the "supply." What RCM modifies is only the identity of the person statutorily obligated to pay the tax on that supply. Since the tax continues to be levied on a "supply" — a concept within the legislative competence conferred by Article 246A — RCM is constitutionally valid. The Supreme Court has consistently held (in pre-GST service tax jurisprudence, e.g., All India Federation of Tax Practitioners v. UOI) that the legislature can designate any person connected with a taxable transaction as the person liable to pay the tax, provided there is a rational nexus between the designated person and the taxable event. Under RCM, the recipient, being a direct party to the supply transaction, has that nexus.

## How RCM Works — The Mechanism
{law_reference: "Section 9(3), 9(4) CGST Act, 2017; Section 31(3)(f) CGST Act; Rules 46, 52 CGST Rules, 2017"}

The operational mechanics of RCM differ from forward charge at every step of the compliance cycle:

**Step 1 — Supply Occurs Without Tax Collection**
The supplier (unregistered or notified category supplier) provides goods or services to the recipient. The supplier does not charge GST on the invoice (or in the case of a notified category, issues a normal invoice but without RCM liability on themselves). No GST flows from recipient to supplier on account of tax.

**Step 2 — Recipient Self-Assesses the Liability**
The registered recipient identifies the supply as falling under Section 9(3) or 9(4). The recipient independently determines: (a) the applicable GST rate, (b) whether CGST+SGST or IGST applies (based on place of supply rules under IGST Act), and (c) the tax amount. This self-assessment is not optional; it is a statutory obligation.

**Step 3 — Recipient Issues Documentary Evidence**
The recipient issues a **self-invoice** under Rule 46 (when the supplier is unregistered) in respect of the inward supply. The recipient also issues a **payment voucher** under Rule 52 at the time of making payment to the supplier. Both documents must comply with all mandatory field requirements and are part of the taxpayer's inward supply records.

**Step 4 — Payment from Cash Ledger Only**
The self-assessed RCM liability is deposited exclusively through the Electronic Cash Ledger. This is a critical constraint: unlike forward-charge ITC which can offset output liability, RCM tax due cannot be offset from the available Input Tax Credit balance. The cash outflow is mandatory. This is expressly provided in the second proviso to Section 49(4) of the CGST Act.

**Step 5 — Reporting in GSTR-3B**
The RCM liability is reported in **Table 3.1(d)** of GSTR-3B ("Inward supplies liable to reverse charge") as output liability. The corresponding ITC, once payment is made, is availed in **Table 4A(3)** of GSTR-3B ("Inward supplies liable to reverse charge").

**Step 6 — ITC Claim After Payment**
The recipient becomes eligible to claim ITC on the RCM amount paid only after actual payment to the government. Section 16(2)(b) requires that tax has been actually paid by the supplier — under RCM, the recipient IS the "supplier" for this purpose, so the condition is satisfied only upon actual remittance. ITC is claimable in the same period of payment, subject to no blocking under Section 17(5) and subject to apportionment under Rule 42/43 if the supply is for mixed purposes.

## Interaction with Blocked ITC and Apportionment
{law_reference: "Section 17(5) CGST Act, 2017; Rule 43 CGST Rules, 2017"}

RCM does not operate in isolation; it intersects with two other critical ITC limitation regimes, and practitioners must evaluate all three layers simultaneously:

**Scenario 1 — RCM Supply Is Also Blocked Under Section 17(5)**
Section 17(5) lists specific goods and services on which ITC is categorically denied — motor vehicles for personal use, food and beverages, club memberships, works contract services for immovable property construction (other than for further supply), etc. If an RCM payment is made on such a blocked supply (e.g., a company pays RCM on catering services received from an unregistered caterer), the RCM tax must still be paid in cash, but the consequent ITC cannot be claimed. The RCM amount becomes an absolute cost. Practitioners must check Section 17(5) before advising clients on ITC recoverability.

**Scenario 2 — RCM Supply Used for Mixed Purposes**
If the supply on which RCM is paid is used partly for taxable supplies and partly for exempt supplies or for personal consumption, the ITC is eligible to the extent used for taxable supply purposes, but must be apportioned as per Rule 43 (for capital goods) or Rule 42 (for inputs and input services). The apportionment calculation uses the ratio of taxable turnover to total turnover for the relevant period. RCM-paid ITC enters the apportionment pool on the same footing as any other input service ITC.

**Scenario 3 — Timing Mismatch Affecting Apportionment**
Because ITC on RCM is claimable only after actual payment (not on accrual of the liability), if payment is made in a month different from the month of supply, the apportionment calculation for Rule 42/43 purposes will apply the ratio of the payment month, not the month of supply. For businesses with seasonal turnover fluctuations, this can result in a higher or lower apportionable fraction than would have applied if the ratio of the supply month were used. Tax counsel must monitor this timing exposure.
