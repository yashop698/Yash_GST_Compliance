## Executive Summary
{topic: "Blocked ITC — Executive Summary"}
{law_reference: "Section 17(5) CGST Act, 2017"}

Section 17(5) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 ("CGST Act") operates as an absolute statutory bar on the availment of input tax credit ("ITC") in respect of a specified catalogue of goods, services, or both. Unlike the proportional ITC reduction mechanism under Sections 17(1) and 17(2) read with Rules 42 and 43, the block under Section 17(5) is categorical and unconditional — once a supply falls within its listed clauses, ITC is denied in its entirety irrespective of the quantum of business use, the commercial purpose of the expenditure, or the degree to which the input contributes to taxable output.

The section currently comprises nine operative clauses — (a), (aa), (b), (c), (d), (e), (f), (g), (h), and (i) — each addressing a distinct category of inward supply on which ITC is either blocked absolutely or blocked subject to enumerated exceptions. The categories span motor vehicles for personal transportation, vessels and aircraft, food and entertainment services, works contract services and construction-related inputs, composition scheme acquisitions, non-resident taxable person acquisitions, goods for personal consumption, lost or destroyed goods and free samples, and tax paid in enforcement proceedings.

The practical impact on businesses is substantial. Companies with large employee welfare programmes — canteens, club memberships, cab services, group insurance — face ITC denials that convert tax into a direct operating cost. Real-estate developers and infrastructure companies must carefully separate construction ITC (blocked) from plant-and-machinery ITC (available). Manufacturers and traders lose ITC on passenger vehicles used for business travel. In aggregate, Section 17(5) represents one of the largest systematic ITC denial mechanisms in the GST framework, and its correct application is critical to accurate GSTR-2B reconciliation, GSTR-3B filing, and Annual Return preparation.

Compliance with Section 17(5) requires three steps: (1) identify whether each inward supply falls within a listed category; (2) verify whether any statutory exception applies; and (3) ensure blocked ITC is not availed in GSTR-3B and is excluded from the ITC pool before Rule 42/43 apportionment is applied to the remaining credits.

## Legislative Intent — Why ITC is Blocked for These Supplies
{law_reference: "Section 17(5) CGST Act, 2017"}
{topic: "Legislative Intent — Blocked ITC"}

Section 17(5) is not arbitrary. The GST Council and Parliament identified four coherent legislative purposes for blocking ITC on the enumerated categories:

### 1. Consumption Tax Logic — GST Taxes Final Consumption, Not Business Inputs

GST is designed as a consumption tax. The ITC mechanism exists to remove the cascading effect of tax on inputs used in the production of taxable outputs — the tax ultimately falls on the final consumer, not on the business. When a supply is consumed internally (personal use, employee benefit, social expense) and does not feed into any taxable output, allowing ITC would effectively subsidise that consumption at the expense of the public revenue. The parliamentary intent is that if a businessman buys a luxury sedan for personal use, the GST on that sedan should be a real cost — exactly as it would be for any private citizen — because the car produces no taxable output that would eventually recover the tax from a downstream consumer.

### 2. Anti-Evasion and Anti-Avoidance

Without Section 17(5), a registered taxpayer could structure personal expenditures as "business expenses" and claim ITC freely. A promoter could route personal club memberships, family vacations, and cosmetic procedures through the company, claim ITC against business output tax, and effectively receive a 28% (or 18%) government subsidy on personal consumption. Section 17(5) closes this avenue by categorically blocking ITC on the categories most susceptible to abuse — entertainment, personal care, club memberships, and travel — regardless of how the expenditure is booked in the accounts.

### 3. Equity and Social Policy — Luxury Items Should Not Be Tax-Subsidised

Certain inputs covered by Section 17(5) are inherently luxury or discretionary in nature — executive vehicles, corporate yachts, outdoor catering for client entertainment, health spas. Parliament's view is that the ITC mechanism should not create a situation where luxury consumption by businesses is effectively subsidised by the State. If a company spends ₹50 lakh on outdoor catering for a product launch and claims ₹9 lakh as ITC, the net cost to the company is ₹41 lakh — an implicit subsidy. Section 17(5)(b)(i) removes this subsidy.

### 4. Revenue Protection — Maintaining the GST Revenue Base

Blocking ITC on personal consumption and entertainment-type expenditures protects the GST revenue base. In the pre-GST era, the corresponding provisions existed under CENVAT Credit Rules (Rules 2(l), 6) and State VAT laws. The GST Council carried forward this protective intent into Section 17(5) to ensure that the ITC mechanism serves its intended purpose — eliminating cascading on business-to-business transactions — without becoming a mechanism for tax-free personal consumption at the business level.

## Philosophical Basis — ITC Follows Output Tax
{topic: "Philosophical Basis — ITC follows Output Tax"}
{law_reference: "Section 16, Section 17, CGST Act, 2017"}

The foundational principle underlying the entire ITC framework is: **"Input tax follows output tax."** ITC is not a refund or a reward — it is a mechanism to ensure that tax paid at one stage of the supply chain is neutralised when tax is collected at the next stage, so that the ultimate tax burden falls only on the final consumer.

This principle explains why:

**Exempt supplies produce no ITC entitlement (Section 17(2)):** If a registered person makes exempt supplies (hospital services, educational services), no GST is collected from the recipient. The ITC mechanism has nothing to neutralise downstream, so there is no economic justification for allowing the input credit. Section 17(2) therefore requires proportional ITC reduction for inputs attributable to exempt supplies.

**Personal consumption produces no ITC entitlement (Section 17(5)(g)):** When goods or services are consumed personally by the proprietor, partner, or director, there is no output supply and no output tax. Allowing ITC would mean the State is funding personal consumption — the very antithesis of a consumption tax.

**Motor vehicles for personal use produce no ITC entitlement (Section 17(5)(a)):** A company car used for the MD's commute produces no taxable output. The car's consumption is personal consumption in economic substance, regardless of formal ownership. There is no output tax chain downstream into which the input tax credit can flow.

**Conversely, where output tax exists, ITC flows:** A car dealer selling motor vehicles collects output GST on every vehicle sold. The input GST paid on the vehicle stock is directly absorbed by the output GST on sale. Allowing ITC to the dealer is perfectly consistent with the "input follows output" principle. This is why Section 17(5)(a) includes an express exception for "further supply of such motor vehicles."

The principle also explains the layered structure of Section 17: Sections 17(1) and 17(2) establish the primary rule (full ITC for taxable-use inputs, proportional ITC for mixed-use inputs). Section 17(5) then carves out an overlay — even if an input would otherwise qualify under Sections 17(1) or 17(2), if it falls within a listed Section 17(5) category, ITC is blocked. The overlay is absolute because Parliament has made a legislative judgment that these specific categories of supplies either never generate sufficient downstream output tax to justify the credit, or are so susceptible to abuse that a categorical rule is preferable to a case-by-case analysis.

## Section 17(5) — Structure and Full Text
{law_reference: "Section 17(5) CGST Act, 2017 as amended by Finance Acts 2018, 2021, 2023"}
{topic: "Section 17(5) — Complete Structure"}

Section 17(5) opens with a non-obstante clause: "Notwithstanding anything contained in sub-section (1) of section 16 and sub-section (1) of section 18..." This is critical — it means Section 17(5) overrides even the primary ITC entitlement provision (Section 16(1)) and the ITC entitlement arising on re-registration or business transfer (Section 18(1)). No supply of goods or services that falls within clauses (a) through (i) can generate an ITC entitlement regardless of how squarely it otherwise satisfies the conditions of Section 16(1) or Section 18(1).

The currently operative text and structure (as of May 2026) is as follows:

### Clause (a) — Motor Vehicles for Transportation of Persons

ITC blocked on motor vehicles for transportation of persons having approved seating capacity of not more than thirteen persons (including the driver).

**Exceptions where ITC is available:**
- (A) Further supply of such motor vehicles (i.e., automobile dealers)
- (B) Transportation of passengers (i.e., taxi operators, cab aggregators, bus operators with ≤13-seater fleet)
- (C) Imparting training on driving such motor vehicles (i.e., motor driving schools)

**Companion block:** ITC also blocked on general insurance, servicing, repair and maintenance of motor vehicles covered under this clause (when the principal vehicle ITC is blocked).

### Clause (aa) — Vessels and Aircraft

ITC blocked on vessels and aircraft.

**Exceptions where ITC is available:**
- (i) For making taxable supplies of:
  - (A) Further supply of such vessels or aircraft
  - (B) Transportation of passengers
  - (C) Imparting training on navigating such vessels
  - (D) Imparting training on flying such aircraft
- (ii) For transportation of goods

### Clause (b) — Specified Services

ITC blocked on:
- (i) Food and beverages, outdoor catering, beauty treatment, health services, cosmetic and plastic surgery, leasing/renting/hiring of motor vehicles/vessels/aircraft referred to in (a) or (aa) except when used for specified purposes, life insurance and health insurance.
  - **Proviso:** ITC available where inward supply of same category is used for outward taxable supply of same category, or as element of composite/mixed supply
- (ii) Membership of a club, health and fitness centre
- (iii) Rent-a-cab, life insurance, health insurance except where:
  - (A) Government notifies these as obligatory for employer to provide under any law in force; or
  - (B) Used for making outward taxable supply of same category
- (iv) Travel benefits extended to employees on vacation such as leave or home travel concession (LTA/LTC)

### Clause (c) — Works Contract Services for Immovable Property Construction

ITC blocked on works contract services when supplied for construction of an immovable property (other than plant and machinery).

**Exception:** ITC available where it is an input service for further supply of works contract service (i.e., sub-contractor receiving works contract service from main contractor for further supply as works contract).

**Explanation (added by Finance Act 2021, retrospective from 01.07.2017):** "Construction" includes re-construction, renovation, additions or alterations or repairs, to the extent of capitalisation, to the said immovable property.

### Clause (d) — Goods/Services for Own Account Construction of Immovable Property

ITC blocked on goods or services or both received by a taxable person for construction of an immovable property (other than plant or machinery) on his own account including when such goods or services or both are used in the course or furtherance of business.

**Same Explanation as (c):** Construction includes reconstruction, renovation, additions, alterations, repairs to the extent capitalised.

### Clause (e) — Composition Scheme Supplies

ITC blocked on goods or services or both on which tax has been paid under Section 10 (Composition Scheme). Composition dealers are not entitled to ITC.

### Clause (f) — Non-Resident Taxable Person Supplies

ITC blocked on goods or services or both received by a non-resident taxable person (NRTP) except on goods imported by him.

### Clause (g) — Personal Consumption

ITC blocked on goods or services or both used for personal consumption.

### Clause (h) — Lost, Stolen, Destroyed, Written Off, Gifts, Free Samples

ITC blocked on goods lost, stolen, destroyed, written off or disposed of by way of gift or free samples.

### Clause (i) — Tax Paid Under Enforcement Provisions

ITC blocked on any tax paid in accordance with sections 74 (fraud/suppression), 129 (detention and seizure), and 130 (confiscation) of the CGST Act.

### Plant and Machinery — Key Exception Across Multiple Clauses

The Explanation appended to Section 17 of the CGST Act defines "plant and machinery" as: apparatus, equipment, and machinery fixed to earth by foundation or structural support that are used for making outward supply of goods or services or both; includes such foundation and structural support; but excludes:
- (i) land, building or any other civil structures
- (ii) telecommunication towers
- (iii) pipelines laid outside the factory premises

## Sections 17(1) to (4) — Context for Section 17(5)
{law_reference: "Section 17(1), 17(2), 17(3), 17(4) CGST Act, 2017"}
{topic: "Sections 17(1)-(4) — Context and Hierarchy"}

Understanding the full architecture of Section 17 is essential because Section 17(5) functions as an overlay on top of the primary ITC framework:

### Section 17(1) — ITC for Exclusive Taxable Supply Use

Where goods or services are used exclusively for making taxable supplies (including zero-rated supplies), the registered person is entitled to full ITC. This is the baseline: 100% credit for 100% taxable-output-generating inputs.

### Section 17(2) — Proportional ITC for Mixed Use

Where goods or services are used partially for making taxable supplies and partly for making exempt supplies, ITC is allowed only to the extent attributable to taxable supplies. The manner of this determination is prescribed in Rules 42 (inputs and input services) and 43 (capital goods).

### Section 17(3) — Definition of "Exempt Supply" for ITC Purposes

For the purposes of Section 17(2), "exempt supply" includes:
- Nil-rated supplies (subject to 0% GST)
- Non-taxable supplies (outside the scope of GST, such as petroleum products, alcohol for human consumption)
- Supplies that are wholly exempt from tax
- Supplies made outside India (considered non-taxable)

Critically, zero-rated supplies (exports and supplies to SEZ units/developers) are **NOT** "exempt supply" for this purpose — ITC on inputs for zero-rated supplies is fully available, and the credit can be refunded.

### Section 17(4) — Aggregate Method for Banks and Financial Institutions

Banks, financial institutions, and non-banking financial companies may opt for a simplified formula: claim 50% of eligible ITC on inputs, input services, and capital goods in each tax period, instead of performing the detailed Rule 42/43 calculation. Once opted, this applies to all credits (except credits on goods and services used exclusively in non-business activities).

### Why This Matters for Section 17(5)

Section 17(5) is an **additional and superior** bar. It is not part of the apportionment mechanism under Sections 17(1)-(4). This has three critical implications:

1. **Section 17(5) applies first:** Before any apportionment calculation under Rule 42 or Rule 43 is performed, blocked ITC must be removed from the pool. An input that is blocked under Section 17(5) is not "common credit" — it is simply not ITC at all.

2. **Section 17(5) overrides Section 17(1):** Even if an input is exclusively used for making taxable supplies (thus qualifying for full ITC under Section 17(1)), if it falls within Section 17(5), ITC is blocked. Example: A taxi company's insurance on its car fleet is exclusively for taxable transportation service — but if the cars have ≤13 seats, ITC on the cars themselves (and their insurance) is blocked under 17(5)(a) unless the exception (clause B) applies. Once the exception applies, ITC becomes available.

3. **No partial recovery within Section 17(5):** Unlike Section 17(2) where some credit is recoverable based on taxable output ratio, Section 17(5) is binary — either the supply falls within the listed categories (ITC blocked 100%) or it doesn't (ITC potentially available subject to other conditions).

## Key Difference: Blocked ITC vs Apportionment
{topic: "Blocked ITC vs Apportionment — Comparison"}
{law_reference: "Section 17(5) vs Section 17(2) read with Rule 42/43 CGST Rules, 2017"}

| Feature | Blocked ITC — Section 17(5) | Apportionment — Section 17(2) / Rule 42/43 |
|---|---|---|
| **Legal basis** | Section 17(5) CGST Act, 2017 | Section 17(2) CGST Act read with Rules 42/43 CGST Rules |
| **Nature of denial** | Absolute categorical bar — specific supply types | Proportional reduction — based on ratio of exempt to total output |
| **Amount of ITC denied** | 100% of ITC on the blocked supply | Only the exempt-output-attributable portion; varies from 0% to 100% |
| **Trigger for denial** | Nature of the inward supply (is it motor vehicle? is it food?) | Mix of taxable vs exempt outputs; changes monthly/annually |
| **Override possible?** | No — unless the supply falls within a statutory exception | Yes — if exempt output reduces, so does the reversal |
| **Retrospective impact** | Section 17(5)(c)/(d) Explanation is retrospective to 01.07.2017 | Prospective; each year's ratio applied to that year's credits |
| **Sequencing** | Applied FIRST — before any apportionment calculation | Applied SECOND — to the pool remaining after Section 17(5) removals |
| **Reporting in GSTR-3B** | Table 4(D)(1) — Ineligible ITC as per Section 17(5) | Table 4(D)(2) — ITC reversed as per Rule 42/43 |
| **Reversal required?** | ITC should not be availed in the first place; if availed, must be reversed | Periodic reversal of proportionate amount required |
| **Remedy on wrong avoidance** | File for refund or amend GSTR-3B with proper justification | Recalculate ratio; claw back any excess reversal |

## Interaction with RCM and Apportionment — Overview
{topic: "Interaction — Blocked ITC, RCM, and Apportionment"}
{law_reference: "Section 9(3), 9(4), Section 17(5) CGST Act, 2017"}

### Interaction 1 — RCM Supply That Is Also Blocked Under Section 17(5)

When a supply received under Reverse Charge Mechanism (RCM) falls within a Section 17(5) blocked category, the consequences are doubly adverse:

- The recipient must pay GST on the supply under RCM (cash outflow — no netting against output ITC)
- The RCM tax paid cannot be claimed as ITC because the supply is blocked under Section 17(5)
- The RCM tax paid is a pure cost — neither creditable nor refundable

**Example:** An employer provides legal security to employees by taking group term life insurance. If the insurance is procured from an insurer registered in another state and RCM applies, the company must pay IGST under RCM. Since group life insurance is blocked under Section 17(5)(b)(iii), the IGST paid under RCM cannot be claimed as ITC. It is an absolute cost.

**Cross-reference:** For full RCM mechanics, see [Topic_1_RCM/06_RCM_Practical_Implementation.md]

### Interaction 2 — Blocked Supplies Excluded from the Rule 42/43 Apportionment Pool

The correct sequencing for ITC computation is:

1. **Step 1:** Identify all inward supplies on which tax has been paid
2. **Step 2:** Remove blocked ITC (Section 17(5)) — these are not part of the credit pool at all
3. **Step 3:** From the remaining pool, identify supplies exclusively for taxable use → full ITC under Section 17(1)
4. **Step 4:** Identify supplies exclusively for exempt use → zero ITC under Section 17(2)
5. **Step 5:** Apply Rule 42/43 to common credits (remaining after Steps 3 and 4)

If blocked ITC is erroneously included in the Rule 42/43 pool, the calculation will be distorted — typically resulting in a lower reversal amount than legally required (since the blocked amount is being treated as partially eligible instead of fully ineligible).

### Interaction 3 — Exempt Supply ITC — Two Routes to Denial

For a supply that relates to exempt output, there are potentially two pathways to ITC denial:

- **Route A (Section 17(5)):** If the inward supply itself falls within a listed category (e.g., food for employees), ITC is blocked 100% by Section 17(5), regardless of any output ratio
- **Route B (Rule 42/43):** If the inward supply does not fall within Section 17(5) but is a common credit used partly for exempt output, only the exempt-attributable portion is denied through the apportionment calculation

These two routes are mutually exclusive for any given supply — a supply is either blocked (Route A) or apportioned (Route B), not both. However, it is possible that a taxpayer has some blocked inputs (Route A) and separately has common inputs that require apportionment (Route B). Both mechanisms operate simultaneously across the total credit pool, but each individual supply falls into only one route.

**[MAHARASHTRA-SPECIFIC]** Maharashtra GST Act, 2017 ("MGST Act") is a mirror legislation to the CGST Act. Section 17(5) of the MGST Act is identical in text and structure to Section 17(5) of the CGST Act. All blocked ITC provisions under the CGST Act apply equally to SGST (Maharashtra). Maharashtra taxpayers claiming SGST ITC must apply the same Section 17(5) analysis independently for SGST credits — i.e., if CGST ITC is blocked on a motor vehicle, the SGST ITC on the same vehicle is equally blocked under Section 17(5) of the MGST Act. There are no Maharashtra-specific carve-outs or expansions to the blocked ITC list as of May 2026.
